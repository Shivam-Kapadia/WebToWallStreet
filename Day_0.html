WHAT CONSULTING ACTUALLY IS

The job, in plain words

Start with the word itself. A consultant is someone a company hires from outside to help them answer a hard question. That's it. That's the whole job description.

Why would a company pay outsiders to think about its own business? Three honest reasons:

No spare people

The company's own staff are busy running the business day to day. Nobody has three months free to study a new market. Consultants are rented brains for a fixed stretch of time.

No experience of this

A firm might enter a new country once in its whole life. A consulting team has done it eleven times for eleven clients. They've seen the shape of the problem before.

No neutral view

Inside a company, every answer has someone's job attached to it. Nobody says "close my department." An outsider can say the unpopular thing.

What you actually deliver

Not a product. Not code. A recommendation — a clear "here's what we think you should do, and here's the evidence" — usually as a slide deck presented to the people who decide.

What the work looks like day to day

Strip away the suits and it's four repeating moves:

  1. Understand the question. Clients often ask the wrong question. "Should we buy this competitor?" might really be "how do we stop losing customers?" Getting this right is half the value.
  2. Break it into pieces. One huge question becomes six small answerable ones. This is the skill the interview tests hardest.
  3. Get evidence for each piece. Data, interviews, industry reports, a spreadsheet model. Small answers, one at a time.
  4. Put it back together as a decision. Six small answers become one recommendation someone can act on Monday morning.
Say it like this

If someone asks what consulting is, don't recite a definition. Say: "A company has a question it can't answer on its own, so it rents a team to answer it properly and come back with a recommendation." That sentence shows you understand the job rather than the job title.

Words you'll hear, in plain English

WordWhat it actually means

WHAT THESE CLUBS DO

NOBE, CUBE, IBC, OTCR

A student consulting club is a small copy of a real consulting firm, run entirely by students, with real clients who don't pay much or don't pay at all. The work is genuine — that's the point.

How a semester runs

CUBE's engagements run twelve to fourteen weeks — one UIUC semester. A team is one Project Manager, three to five Consultants, and an Executive Partner who reviews the work at checkpoints. The PM runs scope and talks to the client. Consultants own workstreams. The partner tears the work apart at the midpoint so it's stronger by the end.

Projects range from go-to-market strategy and market research through to full-stack engineering builds, AI pipelines, hardware prototyping and design systems. Clients are a mix of startups, mid-market firms and Fortune 500 companies.

NOBE splits differently — four project committees (Consulting, Technology, Finance, Product Development), each taking its own client project each semester, plus company-sponsored workshops and panels.

Why this matters for you

Knowing this isn't trivia. When they ask "why us?", a specific answer — naming the workstream structure, the partner review, the committee you'd want — reads as someone who actually looked. Generic enthusiasm reads as filler.

WHAT A CASE INTERVIEW IS

And why it exists at all

A case interview is a conversation where the interviewer gives you a business problem and you solve it out loud, with them, in fifteen to thirty minutes.

It sounds like this:

A typical opening

"Our client is a regional grocery chain. Their profits have fallen fifteen percent over the last two years, while the industry has grown. They've asked us to find out why and recommend what to do. Where would you start?"

Then you talk. You ask questions, they give you numbers, you do arithmetic aloud, you form a view, and you finish with a recommendation. The interviewer is a participant, not an audience — they'll feed you data when you ask for it and nudge you when you wander.

Why clubs use cases instead of normal interviews

Because a normal interview only tells you what someone claims about themselves. A case shows them watching you think in real time, which is exactly the thing the job consists of. In one conversation they see:

  • Whether you can impose order on a messy question
  • Whether you panic when you don't know something
  • Whether you can do arithmetic while a stranger watches
  • Whether you listen or just wait for your turn to talk
  • Whether you take a correction gracefully
  • Whether you'd be pleasant to sit next to at 11pm before a client deadline

The kinds you'll meet

TypeThe question underneathHow common
ProfitabilityProfit fell. Why, and what now?Most common by far
Market entryShould we go into this new market?Very common
Market sizingHow big is this thing? Estimate it.Common, often as a warm-up
PricingWhat should we charge?Fairly common
GrowthRevenue is flat. How do we grow it?Fairly common
AcquisitionShould we buy this company?Less common at club level

Day 1 and Day 2 of this plan cover the machinery for each of these. Today is about the shape they all share.

Sense check

A case is not a test with an answer key. It is closer to a driving test. The examiner doesn't care that you reached the supermarket — they care whether you checked your mirrors, signalled, and stayed calm at the roundabout. Two candidates can reach opposite conclusions and the one with cleaner process wins.

04

STRUCTURE BEATS THE ANSWER

The reframe everything rests on

This is the single most important idea on this page. Everything else is detail hanging off it.

THEY ARE MARKING
HOW YOU THINK
NOT WHERE YOU LAND

Beginners treat a case like a riddle. They hear the prompt, feel the pressure, and start firing guesses: "maybe a competitor cut prices?" — "maybe their costs went up?" — "could it be online shopping?"

Every one of those might be right. It doesn't matter. The candidate has just shown they attack problems by guessing, and no interviewer wants to put that person in front of a client.

What the strong version looks like

Same prompt, different move. The strong candidate says:

The structured opening

"Before I dig in, I'd like to understand the situation better — then I'll lay out how I want to approach it. Profit is revenue minus costs, so the first thing I want to establish is which of those two moved. Once I know that, I can go one level deeper on whichever side is driving it."

Notice what happened. They didn't guess a cause. They built a map of where the answer could possibly be hiding, then said they'd search it in order. Now every question they ask has a reason behind it, and the interviewer can follow their reasoning without being told.

Why interviewers care so much about this

Because on a real project nobody hands you the answer, and you can't guess your way to one over twelve weeks. What actually protects a project is a person who can take a vague question and carve it into pieces that can be checked. That is a rare, trainable, visible skill — and a case interview is the cheapest way to test for it.

Sense check

Guessing is grabbing at one strand and hoping it holds. Structure is anchoring the whole web first, then testing each strand in turn. When one gives way you already know which one, and the rest still holds you.

The habit to build starting now

When you hear any business question — in a case, in the news, in conversation — resist naming a cause. Ask instead: "what are all the places the answer could live?" Do this fifty times and it stops being effort.

THE FOUR-STAGE SKELETON

The shape of every single case

Every case — profitability, entry, pricing, sizing, all of them — runs the same four stages in the same order. Learn the shape once and you stop improvising.

THE CASE SKELETON
CLARIFYUnderstand it
STRUCTUREMap it
ANALYSEWalk the map
CONCLUDEDecide

Rough timing in a twenty-minute case

StageTimeWhat's happening
Clarify2–3 minQuestions that set the edges of the problem
Structure2–3 min30 seconds of silence, then you present your plan
Analyse10–12 minThe bulk. Branch by branch, with maths
Conclude2 minRecommendation, reasons, risk, next step

Don't treat these as stopwatch rules. Treat them as a warning system: if you're eight minutes in and still asking clarifying questions, something has gone wrong.

What each stage is for, in one line

1 — Clarify

Make sure you're solving the right problem. A wrong turn here poisons everything after it, because you'll analyse beautifully in the wrong direction.

2 — Structure

Build and announce the map. This is where you win or lose the interview. Say your plan out loud before you touch it.

3 — Analyse

Walk the map branch by branch. Ask for data, do the arithmetic aloud, close each branch with a one-line verdict.

4 — Conclude

Land it as a decision. Not "they should look into pricing." An actual recommendation someone could act on.

The most common failure

Skipping stage 2. Candidates hear the prompt, ask two questions, then start analysing immediately. It feels efficient and productive. To the interviewer it looks like someone wandering around a building trying doors at random.

06

STAGE 1 &MDASH; CLARIFY

Scoping, not guessing

Before you analyse anything, make sure you understand the problem. Interviewers deliberately leave details out — that's the test. The information is available; you have to ask for it.

The three buckets to pull from

Objective & scope

What counts as success, and where are the edges? "When you say sales, do you mean revenue or units?" "Is this the whole company or one division?" "Is there a target we're aiming at?"

Client profile

What is this business, actually? "What do they sell and who buys it?" "How do they make money?" "How long have they been operating?"

The number itself

Pin down the fact in the prompt. "Since when has this been happening?" "Is that fall against last year, or against a plan?" "Gradual or sudden?"

Ask two to four of these, front-loaded, before you build your structure. Not scattered through the case. Too many and you look like you're stalling.

The mistake that costs the most marks

Here is the distinction almost every beginner gets wrong, including you the first time:

A clarifier names a boundary

"Is the decline in one region or across all of the US?"
This tells you where to look. It doesn't propose a cause.

A hypothesis names a cause

"Could a competitor be pricing us out?"
This is a guess wearing a question mark. It belongs in Analyse, not here.

Why it reads badly

A hypothesis in the clarify stage tells the interviewer you started guessing before you built a structure — the exact failure mode from section 04. The idea might be excellent. The timing makes it look like flailing.

The test is simple. Ask yourself: does this question narrow where I search, or does it propose an answer? Narrowing is a clarifier. Proposing is a hypothesis. Save every hypothesis for stage 3, where it becomes a strength.

DRILL — CLARIFIER OR HYPOTHESIS?

Prompt: a national pizza chain's profits fell 20% last year. Sort each question.

Score: 0 / 10
Closing the stage cleanly

When you've asked enough, say so — don't just trail off. "That's helpful, thank you. Could I take thirty seconds to put my thoughts together?" Then take it. Silence while you write is completely normal and expected.

STAGE 2 &MDASH; STRUCTURE

Building the map out loud

You've taken your thirty seconds. Now you present the map. This is the highest-value ninety seconds of the whole interview.

The one rule that separates good from bad here

Never say this

"I'm going to use the profitability framework."
Naming a framework tells the interviewer you memorised a template and are about to apply it whether it fits or not.

Say this instead

"Profit is revenue minus costs, so I'd first establish which side moved. Then I'd go a level deeper on that side. Then I'd check whether anything outside the company explains it."

Same structure. Utterly different impression. The second version describes what you are going to do, in the language of this specific business. The first describes a tool you own.

The shape of a good structure statement

  1. Announce the arc. "I'd like to look at three areas." Give the number first — it lets the interviewer follow.
  2. Name each area in plain words. Not "internal and external factors" if you can say "what's happening inside the stores, and what's happening in the market around them."
  3. Say where you'll start and why. "I'd start with costs, since the prompt mentioned margins specifically."
  4. Then actually start there. Candidates announce a plan and immediately abandon it. The interviewer notices.
A full worked example

"I'd like to break this into three parts. First, whether the profit fall came from revenue or from costs — that tells us which half of the business to look at. Second, within whichever one it is, whether it's a price problem or a volume problem. Third, whether anything in the wider market — a new competitor, a shift in what customers want — explains what we find. I'd like to start with the first, because it narrows the search fastest. Could I ask how revenue has moved over the two years?"

That is roughly forty seconds of speech and it does an enormous amount of work: it shows a plan, gives a reason for the order, and ends by driving the case forward with a specific question.

Sense check

Your structure is the web you anchor before you step out onto it. Anchor it badly and every move afterwards is a scramble. Thirty seconds of silence spent anchoring properly buys you ten minutes of calm.

Write it so a stranger could read it

Your paper matters. Keep one clean sheet with your structure on it — branches, headings, room to fill in numbers. Interviewers sometimes glance at it. A tidy sheet reinforces everything you're claiming about yourself; a scribbled mess undercuts it.

CHECKPOINT 07

STAGE 3 &MDASH; ANALYSE

Walking the map, branch by branch

Now you walk the map you just drew. This is ten to twelve minutes — the bulk of the case — and it's where most of the marks actually get awarded.

The two questions to keep asking

Almost every useful insight in a case comes from a comparison. A number on its own means nothing. Two comparisons unlock nearly everything:

Compared to before

"How has this moved over time?" If costs are 30% of revenue, that's neither good nor bad — until you learn they were 22% two years ago.

Compared to others

"How does this compare to competitors?" If everyone's margins fell, it's an industry problem. If only ours fell, it's a company problem. Completely different recommendations.

Do the arithmetic out loud

Don't go quiet and scribble. Narrate it, so the interviewer can follow and correct you before you go far wrong:

Narrating a calculation

"So revenue was 2 billion and is now 180 — that's a fall of 20, so 10 percent. Costs went from 150 to 160, up about 7 percent. So profit went from 50 down to 20. Revenue fell, but the bigger story is that both moved against us at once."

Two habits inside that: round aggressively and ask permission to round. "May I use 30% rather than 31%?" is always granted and always makes you look composed rather than sloppy.

Say the "so what" after every number

This is the difference between arithmetic and consulting. A calculation with no interpretation scores close to nothing.

Number only

"Variable cost per unit went up 20 percent."
True. Silent. The interviewer is waiting.

Number plus meaning

"Variable cost per unit went up 20 percent while price stayed flat — so the margin squeeze is coming from inputs, not from discounting. That points me at suppliers."

Close each branch before moving on

When you finish a branch, give it a one-line verdict and say where you're going next. This is called a mini-synthesis, and it's what makes an interview sound like a conversation instead of a checklist being read aloud.

Closing a branch

"So costs aren't the story — they've actually grown slower than inflation. That points me back to the revenue side, and specifically to volume. Could I look at how many units we sold each year?"

When you get stuck

You will. Everyone does. The recovery matters more than the stall.

  • Say what you know, then what you'd want. "I've established it's a volume problem and not a price problem. What I'd want next is whether we're losing existing customers or failing to win new ones."
  • Ask for a moment. "Could I take ten seconds to think?" Perfectly acceptable. Better than filling silence with noise.
  • Take the hint. Interviewers drop hints when they see you drifting — "that's interesting, but what about the supply side?" is not conversation. It is a rescue rope. Grab it. Candidates who dig in on their own path after a hint score badly.
Sense check

Each branch you close is a strand you've tested and found solid or slack. Closing branches out loud is how the interviewer knows you're testing rather than wandering. An untested web looks identical to a tested one — until you put weight on it.

CHECKPOINT 08
  • Do you compare every number to something — before, or others?
  • Do you say the "so what" after every calculation, without being asked?
  • Can you close a branch and signpost the next one in one sentence?

STAGE 4 &MDASH; CONCLUDE

Landing it like a decision

Two minutes left. This is the part candidates most often fumble, because they're tired and they let the case fade out instead of landing it.

RECOMMENDATION
+ 2–3 REASONS
+ 1 RISK OR NEXT STEP

Lead with the answer

Don't build up to it. Don't recap the journey. State the recommendation in the first sentence, then support it. Senior people want the conclusion first — if they want the reasoning they'll ask.

Weak ending

"So we looked at revenue and costs, and we found some things on the cost side, and I think the client should probably look into their pricing strategy and maybe consider their supplier relationships."

Strong ending

"I'd recommend renegotiating the two largest supply contracts. Input costs drove 80 percent of the margin fall, our prices are already at the market ceiling, and volume is healthy. The risk is supplier concentration — if these two walk, we have no alternative, so I'd want a backup supplier identified first."

Why the risk line matters so much

Adding a risk is the cheapest way to look senior. It says: I know my recommendation isn't free, and I've thought about what could go wrong. Almost no beginner does this, so it stands out immediately.

If you can't think of a risk, offer a next step instead — "the next thing I'd want to check is whether the volume figures hold up by region." Same effect: it shows the thinking continues past the answer.

Rules for the last two minutes

  • Be concrete. "Raise price 8% on the premium line, where demand is least sensitive" beats "consider pricing strategy." A recommendation someone can act on Monday.
  • Sanity-check any number before you say it. A wrong figure in the final line undoes twenty good minutes.
  • Never trail off. Finish on a full stop, not a rising tone. If you're unsure, say the recommendation anyway — a confident wrong answer with clear reasoning scores far better than no answer.
  • Don't hedge into mush. "It depends" is not a conclusion. Pick a side and name the condition under which you'd change your mind.
Sense check

The whole case has been you spinning strands. The recommendation is the moment you put weight on the web and let go of the ledge. Do it with your voice steady, even if privately you're only 70% sure. Nobody is ever 100% sure — that's why it's a recommendation and not a fact.

CHECKPOINT 09
  • Does your first sentence contain the actual recommendation?
  • Do you give two or three reasons, not seven?
  • Do you add a risk or a next step every single time?
  • Can you end on a full stop?

MECE

No overlaps, no gaps

MECE stands for Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. Consultants say it constantly. Translated into plain English it means:

NO OVERLAPS
NO GAPS

Mutually exclusive — your categories don't bleed into each other. Nothing belongs in two of them.
Collectively exhaustive — between them, your categories cover everything. Nothing is left out.

A broken split, and why it's broken

Question: why are our sales down? The beginner's list:

Not MECE

Pricing  ·  Marketing  ·  The economy  ·  Competitors  ·  Our website

Overlaps: the website is part of marketing. A competitor cutting prices sits in both "competitors" and "pricing."
Gaps: nothing here covers distribution, product quality, or the sales team. If the answer lives there, this candidate will never find it.

Notice the real damage: an overlap wastes time and confuses the listener, but a gap means the answer can be somewhere you never look. Gaps are the dangerous half.

The same question, done properly

WHY ARE SALES DOWN?
INSIDE THE COMPANYThings we control
Product — quality, range
Price — level, discounts
Getting it to people — channels, stock
Telling people — marketing, sales team
OUTSIDE THE COMPANYThings we don't
Customers — what they want changed
Competitors — new, cheaper, better
The market — economy, regulation

Two top-level buckets, no overlap, and together they genuinely cover everything — a cause is either something the company controls or something it doesn't. There's no third option. That's what makes it safe.

Splits that are MECE by construction

Some splits can't fail, because they're built on a real dividing line. Reach for these when you're under pressure:

SplitWhy it can't leak
Internal / ExternalYou either control it or you don't
Revenue / CostsMoney comes in or goes out
Price / VolumeRevenue is only ever these two multiplied
Fixed / VariableA cost either moves with output or doesn't
New / Existing customersSomeone has bought before, or hasn't
Supply / DemandEither we can't make it or they won't buy it
The honest caveat

Perfect MECE is an aim, not a religion. Real problems are messy and a slightly imperfect structure said with confidence beats a perfect one you're still building in silence. Aim for no gaps first — overlaps cost you a little elegance, gaps cost you the answer.

  • Can you spot the overlap and the gap in a bad list?
  • Do you know why gaps are worse than overlaps?

ISSUE TREES

How to split anything

An issue tree is what you get when you apply MECE over and over. Start with the big question, split it into parts, split those parts, and keep going until you hit something you can actually check with a number.

How far down to go

Until the bottom of a branch is testable — something you could ask the interviewer for. "Costs" isn't testable. "Cost per delivery" is. Usually that's two or three levels. Going deeper burns time; stopping shallower leaves you with nothing to ask about.

The profitability tree

This one is worth memorising cold, because profitability is the most common case type you'll meet. Everything hangs off one equation:

PROFIT = REVENUE − COSTS
PROFIT
REVENUEMoney coming in
Price — per unit, mix, discounts
Quantity — volume, share, market size
COSTSMoney going out
Fixed — rent, salaries, equipment
Variable — materials, labour, shipping

Using it as a search, not a recital

The tree isn't something you read out. It's something you search in order, and each question you ask should cut the search space roughly in half:

  1. "Has revenue fallen, or have costs risen?" — kills half the tree in one question.
  2. "Is that a price problem or a volume problem?" — halves it again.
  3. "Is the volume drop in existing customers or new ones?" — now you're somewhere specific.

Three questions and you've localised the issue, while a weaker candidate is still listing possibilities. This is what people mean when they say someone "drives the case."

Sense check

A tree isn't decoration and it isn't a checklist to read aloud. It's a search grid. You're not showing the interviewer everywhere the answer could be — you're eliminating regions until only one is left.

When the tree doesn't crack it

Sometimes you split revenue and costs and neither side gives a clean answer. The escape hatch is to walk the value chain instead — the actual sequence of steps the business runs to deliver its product:

SUPPLIERS → MAKING IT → STORING IT → MOVING IT → SELLING IT → AFTER-SALE

Walk each step and ask what could go wrong there. This catches operational problems that a revenue-and-costs split can hide — a warehouse bottleneck, a supplier who became unreliable, a delivery step that quietly got expensive.

Practise this way

Take any business question and draw the tree on paper with no timer. Then interrogate your own drawing: does anything overlap? Did I miss anything? Is the bottom of each branch something I could actually ask for a number on? Ten trees drawn slowly beats fifty read quickly.

CHECKPOINT 11
  • Can you draw the profitability tree from memory, three levels deep?
  • Do you know how far down to split — and why?
  • Can you name three questions that each halve the search space?

SIZING A MARKET

Guessing a number properly

"How many umbrellas are sold in Chicago each year?" You don't know. Nobody knows. That's fine — nobody expects you to.

What's being tested is whether you can build an estimate out of things you do know, stay organised, and do arithmetic calmly while someone watches. The final number barely matters. The path to it is the whole mark.

The method

  1. Start from a number you're confident about. Usually a population. City, country, households.
  2. Apply filters, one at a time. Each filter cuts the group down. Say each one out loud as you apply it.
  3. Convert to the thing being asked. People → buyers → purchases per year.
  4. Sanity-check the result. Does it feel plausible? If not, say so and name which assumption you'd revisit.

Worked example

StepReasoningNumber
PopulationChicago, roughly3,000,000
Adults who'd buy their ownSay 60% — excludes young children1,800,000
Who own an umbrella at allSay 80% — it rains there1,440,000
Replacement rateLost, broken, left in a cab — every 2 years÷ 2
Annual purchasesPlus tourists and impulse buys, round up~750,000

The four habits that get you the marks

Round hard

Use 3 million, never 2,746,388. Round numbers are faster, less error-prone, and signal that you understand this is an estimate.

Say every assumption

"I'm assuming 60% are adults — is that reasonable?" If the interviewer corrects you, that's a gift, not a failure. They're steering you.

State your unit and stay in it

"I'll work in millions." Most errors in market sizing are unit slips, not arithmetic slips.

Sanity-check at the end

"That's about one umbrella per four people per year, which feels about right." This single sentence separates you from most candidates.

The trap

Going quiet to do the arithmetic. If you stop narrating, the interviewer has no idea whether you're calculating or frozen — and they can't help you. Talk through every step, even the boring ones.

CHECKPOINT 12
  • Can you name the four steps of the method?
  • Do you round to clean numbers automatically?
  • Do you say assumptions out loud and invite correction?
  • Do you sanity-check the final number?

SIGNPOSTING

Saying where you are

Signposting means saying where you are before you go there. It costs you four seconds and it changes how organised you sound more than any other single habit.

The interviewer is taking notes and cannot read your mind. Without signposting, a good answer can sound like wandering. With it, even a stumbling answer sounds controlled.

The phrasebook

MomentWhat to say
Confirming the problem"So the client is X, the issue is Y, and we want Z. Have I got that right?"
Buying thinking time"Could I take thirty seconds to structure my thoughts?"
Presenting structure"I'd like to look at three areas. First… second… third…"
Starting a branch"Let me start with the cost side."
Asking for data"Do we have a figure for how sales split by region?"
Before calculating"Let me work through that — I'll do it out loud."
Rounding"May I round that to 30% to keep it clean?"
Closing a branch"So costs aren't the driver here. Let me move to volume."
Recovering from an error"Let me correct that — the figure is 42, not 24. So that changes the picture to…"
Taking a hint"That's a good point — let me look at the supply side instead."
Concluding"I'd recommend X. Three reasons. The main risk is Y."
How to practise it

Say these aloud until they're automatic. In the interview you'll be thinking hard about the problem — there's no spare capacity to also invent phrasing. Pre-loaded sentences free up your brain for the actual work.

Sense check

Signposting is a line of web trailing behind you. The interviewer can see exactly where you've been and where you're heading. Without it, you might be moving brilliantly — but from the outside it looks like flailing in the dark.

DO'S, DON'TS & SCORING

What they're marking you on

Do

  • Build rapport in the opening seconds. Smile, greet them properly. They're deciding whether they'd enjoy working with you from the first moment.
  • Stay composed when a number comes out wrong. Own it, correct it, move on. One clean correction. Not three apologies.
  • Keep your working sheet clean enough that a stranger could follow it.
  • Ask for what you need. Data, a moment, a repeat of a figure. Asking is expected, not weakness.
  • Watch for hints and take them. They're a rescue rope, not conversation.
  • Say the "so what" after every number.

Don't

  • Don't assume any fact you weren't given. If you need it, ask. Inventing data is the fastest way to lose credibility.
  • Don't interrupt. The detail they mention once is often the key to the case.
  • Don't treat a framework as gospel. An acquisition can fix a profitability problem and no framework will hand you that — judgment fills the gaps.
  • Don't panic visibly. Everyone stalls. The recovery is what's marked.
  • Don't guess causes before you've built a structure.
  • Don't trail off at the end.

The one that surprises people

Coachability. When an interviewer pushes back, weak candidates defend their answer. Strong ones say "that's a fair point, let me reconsider" — and then genuinely change course.
