01

THE PROFIT EQUATION

The spine that every case hangs off

A business does two things: it takes money in, and it pays money out. What's left is profit. So the starting point is embarrassingly simple:

PROFIT = REVENUE − COSTS

Everyone knows this. It's also useless on its own, because "revenue went down" isn't a finding — it's a restatement of the problem. What separates a candidate who sounds sharp from one who sounds lost is how far they can break this equation apart before they start asking questions.

Step one: split revenue and costs

Revenue is just how many things you sold, times what you charged for each one. Costs come in two flavours — costs that grow with each sale, and costs that sit there regardless. So:

PROFIT = (VOLUME × PRICE)
− (VOLUME × VARIABLE COST PER UNIT)
− FIXED COSTS

Read that as four separate dials: how many you sold, what you charged, what each one cost you, and the overheads. Every profitability case is a hunt for which of those four dials moved. There is no fifth option. That is genuinely the whole game, and knowing it means you're never staring blankly at a prompt again.

Step two: the version you should actually write down

Volume appears twice above, so pull it out:

PROFIT = VOLUME × (PRICE − VARIABLE COST) − FIXED COSTS

This is the form to memorise. It's cleaner, and it hands you the single most useful quantity in case interviews — the bracket, Price − Variable Cost, which has a name we'll get to in module 03.

The mistake almost every beginner makes

Sooner or later someone writes the equation like this instead:

Sense checkA web only holds if every strand is anchored somewhere different. The moment two strands pull on the same anchor, the whole thing sags in one direction and you can't tell which strand failed. That is exactly what happens when fixed-cost-per-unit sits inside the bracket — two dials wired to one knob.

PROFIT = VOLUME × (PRICE − VAR. COST − FIXED COST PER UNIT)

The maths adds up fine. The thinking is broken, and here's why that matters.

Fixed cost per unit depends on volume. Rent is $50,000 a month whether you sell 1,000 units or 10,000. So fixed-cost-per-unit is $50 in the first case and $5 in the second. The moment volume moves, that term moves too — you now have two dials wired to the same knob.

Here's what goes wrong. Say sales drop. Suddenly your "fixed cost per unit" number shoots up — but nothing about your rent changed. You announce "costs are the problem" when the real problem was that you sold less. You've blamed the wrong dial.

Why this earns points

Keeping overheads as one separate lump means all four dials move on their own. So when you check them one at a time, each answer is clean. Interviewers notice this immediately — it's a small technical choice that signals you understand why the structure is the shape it is, not just that you memorised it.

What each dial actually means in the real world

DialPlain meaningWhere the answer usually hides
VolumeHow many units walked out the doorDemand, competition, distribution, stockouts
PriceWhat each one sold forDiscounting, competitive pressure, mix (module 05)
Variable costCost of each unit you soldSupplier prices, input inflation, wastage
Fixed costsOverhead that exists regardlessRent, salaries, new stores, marketing spend

The one adaptation worth knowing

Watch for one word swap. Profit is the rupees you keep. Profitability is what percentage of your sales you keep. A company can earn more rupees while keeping a smaller percentage — it got bigger but got worse at converting sales into profit. When you hear "our margins have fallen," that's the percentage, and module 05 is where you go.

THE PROFITABILITY TREE

The most common case type, solved step by step

Roughly half of all cases you'll get sound like this: "Our client's profits are down. Why, and what should they do?"

Module 01 gave you the equation. This module gives you the map you walk — the actual sequence of questions, in order, that takes you from "profits are down" to a specific cause you can act on. Learn this one properly and you will never freeze at the start of a case again.

The whole tree in one picture

Profit is money in minus money out. So there are only two places the problem can be. Split it, then keep splitting.

PROFIT IS DOWN
MONEY IN FELL
(revenue)
We sold fewer things
(volume down)
We charged less per thing
(price down)
MONEY OUT ROSE
(costs)
Cost of each thing sold rose
(variable costs)
Overheads rose
(fixed costs)

That's it. Four boxes at the bottom. Every profitability case ends up in one or two of them. Your job is to find out which, and the way you do that is by asking for numbers and eliminating.

The move that saves you every time

Ask for revenue and total costs separately, for this year and last year. In one question you've cut the tree in half. If revenue is flat and costs rose, you never have to look at the revenue side at all — say that out loud and move on. Interviewers love watching a candidate eliminate.

Going down the revenue side

Say you've established revenue fell. Revenue is just:

REVENUE = HOW MANY WE SOLD × PRICE EACH

So ask which one moved. And each has its own set of causes.

If we sold fewer things

There are only three reasons a business sells less, and they're worth memorising because they cover everything:

  • The whole market shrank. Everyone is selling less, not just us. Fewer people want this kind of product at all. If this is the answer, our competitors are hurting too — so ask about market size and competitor performance.
  • The market is fine but we lost share. Customers are still buying, just from someone else. Ask what changed — a new competitor, a price move, a product problem, worse service.
  • We couldn't supply it. Demand was there and we couldn't meet it. Stockouts, a factory problem, a supplier that failed, not enough staff. Easy to forget, and it's occasionally the whole answer.

Another way to slice the same thing, useful for shops and services:

HOW MANY WE SOLD =
NUMBER OF CUSTOMERS × HOW OFTEN THEY BUY
× HOW MUCH THEY BUY EACH TIME

Fewer customers is a very different problem from the same customers visiting less often, which is different again from them visiting just as much but putting less in the basket. Three different fixes. Ask which one moved.

If we charged less per thing

  • We deliberately cut prices — a promotion, a discount war, a decision to chase volume
  • We were forced down — a competitor undercut us and we had to follow
  • We didn't change prices at all, but the average fell anyway — because customers shifted toward our cheaper products. This is the mix effect, covered in module 06. Watch for it, because everyone misses it

Going down the cost side

Same logic. Split costs into the two kinds first, because they behave completely differently.

Costs that grow with every sale

Materials, stock bought from suppliers, packaging, delivery per order, payment processing fees. If these went up, either:

  • Suppliers raised their prices — an input got more expensive, a currency moved, a shortage happened
  • We're wasting more — spoilage, damage, returns, rework, theft
  • We changed what we buy — switched to a pricier supplier or a better-quality input
  • We lost buying power — we're ordering smaller quantities than before, so we lost the bulk discount

Costs that stay the same regardless

Rent, salaried staff, equipment, insurance, software, marketing budget. If these went up:

  • We added capacity — new stores, a new warehouse, more head office staff
  • Existing costs got more expensive — rent renewal, salary rises, an insurance jump
  • We spent more on something optional — a big marketing campaign, a consulting project, new software
Careful — the trap here

Total fixed costs going up is not automatically bad. If a company opened 10 new stores, of course rent went up. The real question is whether those stores are earning enough to justify it. Always ask: did the extra cost buy us extra revenue, and was it enough?

The two questions to ask at every single box

Whichever branch you're on, a number on its own tells you nothing. $4 per unit is neither good nor bad. So at every step, ask:

Compared to before

Was it different last year? Two years ago? This tells you what changed.

Compared to competitors

Do rivals have the same number? This tells you whether it's our problem or the industry's.

Those two comparisons are the engine of the whole case. If our cost per unit rose from $3 to $4 and competitors are still at $3, that's our problem to fix. If competitors also went to $4, it's an industry-wide input cost rise and the conversation becomes "can we pass it on to customers?" — an entirely different recommendation.

Watch a full case run through it

A supermarket chain's profits fell 20% last year. Here's the whole thing, question by question.

You askThey sayWhat you now know
Did revenue fall, or did costs rise?Revenue is up 5%. Costs are up 12%.Revenue side is fine. Kill that whole branch. It's a cost problem.
Which costs — cost of goods, or overheads?Cost of goods rose 4%. Overheads rose 30%.Suppliers aren't the issue. It's overheads.
Which overheads specifically?Rent is up 45%. Everything else is flat.Narrowed to one line item.

Notice what happened. Six questions, and each one killed a branch rather than wandering. You never touched pricing, never touched competitors, never touched the revenue side — because question one ruled it out. That's what walking a tree looks like, and it's why structure beats cleverness.

Your recommendation

"The profit decline is driven by the 10 new stores. They carry comparable rent to existing stores but generate a fifth of the revenue, because they opened in lower-footfall towns. I'd recommend pausing further small-town openings until we understand the unit economics, and reviewing whether these 10 can reach breakeven — if a store can't cover its rent within a defined window, we should exit the lease. I'd want to check two things: how long a new store normally takes to mature, and whether the lease terms allow early exit."

Read that back. It names the cause, gives the numbers, gives an action, and flags what you'd verify. That is a complete case answer, and every part of it came from walking the tree.

The escape hatch, for when the tree runs out

Sometimes you narrow down to "we sold fewer units" and the interviewer won't tell you why. When the tree stops giving you branches, walk the path the product takes to reach a customer instead:

  1. Can we make it? Production problems, supply shortages, capacity limits
  2. Can we get it in front of people? Are shops stocking it? Are they pushing it? Do they earn enough selling ours versus a rival's? Is it well placed on the shelf?
  3. Do people want it? Has demand genuinely fallen — taste changed, a substitute appeared, our reputation slipped?

Those three cover everything between the factory and the customer, they don't overlap, and each one points somewhere concrete. Keep it in your back pocket.

  • Volume fell 10%. Give one question that tests each of the three volume causes.

THE TWO KINDS OF COST

Costs that grow when you sell more, and costs that don't

Every cost a business has falls into one of two piles. This sounds boring. It is the single most useful sorting you will do, because the two piles behave in completely opposite ways and mixing them up leads you to the wrong answer.

Pile one: costs that grow with each sale

You run a coffee cart. Every cup you sell uses coffee beans, milk, sugar, and a paper cup. Sell 100 cups, you use 100 cups' worth. Sell zero, you use nothing.

These are called variable costs — "variable" because the amount you spend varies with how much you sell. Materials, stock you buy from suppliers, packaging, delivery charges per order.

Pile two: costs that stay put

Same coffee cart. You pay $1,000 a month for the spot you're standing on. Sell 5,000 cups or sell nothing — you still owe $1,000.

These are called fixed costs — "fixed" because they don't move when your sales move. Rent, salaried staff, machinery, insurance, software, the electricity bill.

Not sure which pile? One question sorts it

IF I SOLD ONE FEWER TODAY,
WOULD THIS COST GO DOWN?

Yes → it grows with sales. No → it stays put. That's the whole test.

CostSell one less — does it drop?Which pile
Milk for the coffeeYes, you use less milkGrows with sales
Paper cupYes, one fewer cup usedGrows with sales
Rent for the cartNo, still $1,000Stays put
Your helper's monthly salaryNo, you still pay himStays put
Festival banner you printedNo, already paid forStays put

Why this actually matters — two very different businesses

Here's where it stops being definitions and starts being useful. Two businesses can make the same money today and behave like completely different animals, purely because of which pile their costs sit in.

Business A: mostly fixed costs

A cinema. The screen, the projector, the building, the staff — all paid for whether 5 people show up or 200. The cost of letting one more person in is basically zero. Nobody uses up anything.

What this means:

  • They are desperate to fill seats, because every extra seat sold is almost pure profit
  • Profit swings wildly. A slow month doesn't reduce their costs at all, so it goes straight to losses
  • They will slash prices at the last minute — a $15 ticket for an empty seat is $15 more than nothing. Same reason airlines and hotels discount unsold inventory

Airlines, gyms, hotels, cinemas, phone networks, software companies all live here.

Business B: mostly costs that grow with sales

A grocery shop. Its biggest expense by far is buying the goods it resells. Sell twice as much, you buy twice as much stock.

What this means:

  • Much more stable. A slow month also means you bought less stock, so costs fall too
  • But growth doesn't fix margins. Double your sales and you roughly double your costs — the percentage you keep barely improves
  • To get better, they need cheaper buying or a better product mix, not just more volume

Shops, distributors, restaurants, most service businesses live here.

Say this in an interview

"Before I go further — is most of this client's cost base fixed or variable? Because if it's mostly fixed, a drop in sales hurts profit much more than the revenue number suggests, and that changes where I'd look first."

A trick for when a fixed cost looks like the problem

Say rent went up and rent looks like the culprit. A beginner says "reduce the rent." That's not advice — you can't just ask a landlord nicely.

Instead, split the cost into two smaller questions:

RENT COST PER SALE =
WHAT WE PAY PER SQUARE FOOT
÷ HOW MUCH WE SELL PER SQUARE FOOT

Now instead of one blob you have two testable things:

  • Did the landlord charge more per square foot? → renegotiate, or move
  • Or is the same space just selling less? → fix the layout, the range, the footfall

Completely different problems, completely different fixes.

See it work

A chain grows from 30 stores to 40. Floor space up 33%. Total rent up 33%. Looks bad, right?

But revenue doubled. So each square foot is now selling far more than before, and the rent cost per sale actually fell. Rent is helping profitability, not hurting it. If you'd stopped at "total rent up 33%" you'd have chased entirely the wrong thing.

This same split works on any fixed cost:

  • Staff cost → what we pay per person ÷ how much each person produces
  • Machine cost → cost per machine ÷ units each machine makes
  • Marketing → total spend ÷ customers it brought in

Sense checkFalling and swinging are different physics. A mostly-fixed-cost business is swinging — committed to the arc, and it either reaches the next anchor or it doesn't. A mostly-variable-cost business is walking — slower, but it can stop at any step without consequence. Work out which one your client is doing before you recommend a leap.

CHECKPOINT
  • Sort these into the two piles: packaging, shop manager's salary, electricity, supplier invoice, delivery fuel, yearly software licence.
  • Is a cinema mostly fixed or mostly variable? What does that predict about ticket prices on a quiet Tuesday afternoon?
  • "Our salary costs are too high." Split that into two smaller questions you could actually test.

CONTRIBUTION MARGIN & BREAKEVEN

The calculation you will do in almost every case

Contribution margin, from scratch

Remember the bracket from module 01. It has a name:

CONTRIBUTION MARGIN =
PRICE − VARIABLE COST PER UNIT

It's called contribution because that's literally what it does. Every sale hands you this much money toward paying off your overheads. Once the overheads are fully paid, every sale after that is pure profit.

Picture it as a bucket. Fixed costs are a hole in the bottom, $20,000 deep. Each sale pours in $2. You need to pour in 10,000 sales' worth before the bucket starts holding anything at all.

Breakeven

BREAKEVEN VOLUME =
FIXED COSTS ÷ CONTRIBUTION MARGIN

That's it. Fixed costs of $20,000 per month, contribution of $2 per order → you need 10,000 orders a month, roughly 333 a day, just to reach zero.

Now watch how much analytical leverage that one number gives you. The interviewer says: "current volume is 280 orders a day." You immediately know:

  • They're losing money — 280 is below 333
  • The gap is 53 orders a day, about 19% short
  • You can close it from either side: push volume up, or push contribution margin up so the bar itself drops

Say variable costs fall by $0.50 through better operations. Contribution becomes $2.50. New breakeven = $20,000 ÷ $2.50 = 8,000 orders, or ~267 a day. Suddenly 280 orders is above breakeven and the business is profitable — without selling a single extra unit.

That is the entire logic of a huge share of consulting recommendations, and you can now generate it yourself.

Sense checkBreakeven is where the strand goes taut. Below it, you are still falling and nothing you do is holding weight. Above it, every additional order is pure lift. Know exactly where that point is before you argue about anything else — the number reframes the whole case in one line.

Two things beginners get wrong

Confusing contribution margin with profit margin. Contribution margin ignores fixed costs entirely — it's per-unit and it's about covering overhead. Profit margin is after everything. A product with a healthy $2 contribution can still lose money if the business only sells 100 of them a month.

Assuming overheads stay flat forever. They stay flat within limits. Sell enough and you need a second warehouse — then overheads jump in one big step. If a case has you tripling sales, ask whether the current setup can even handle it, or your breakeven maths quietly stops being true.

Say this in an interview

"Contribution per order is $2, and fixed costs are $20,000 a month, so breakeven is around 333 orders a day against current volume of 280. So we're about 19% short — and I'd want to look at both closing that volume gap and lifting contribution, since a $0.50 cut in variable cost alone moves breakeven to 267."

CHECKPOINT
  • Price $50, variable cost $35, fixed costs $150,000/year. Breakeven units?
  • If you could either raise price $2.50 or cut variable cost $2.50 — which helps contribution more? (Trick question. Why?)
  • Explain the bucket metaphor to someone who has never heard of any of this.
05

MARGINS & COGS

The three-step staircase that tells you where the bleeding is

What "margin" actually means

Margin is profit expressed as a percentage of revenue. Not in rupees — as a percentage.

Sell $10 of stuff, keep $2 of it as profit → 20% margin.

Why use a percentage instead of just rupees? Because it tells you how good a business is, separately from how big it is. A company making $1 million of profit on $100 million of revenue (1% margin) and one making $1 million on $5 million of revenue (20% margin) earned identical rupees — but the second is a far healthier business. The second one survives a bad year. The first is one bad month away from losing money.

This is also why you can't compare margins across industries. Grocery chains keep 1–3% and that's completely normal. Software companies keep 70%+ and that's also normal. Saying "their margin is only 2%, that's terrible" about a supermarket marks you instantly as new.

What COGS means

Cost of Goods Sold — the direct cost of the specific things you actually sold.

For a grocery chain, COGS is what the chain paid its suppliers for the milk, bread and rice that left the store. Bought a carton of milk at $5, sold it at $6.50 → the $5 is COGS.

The key word is direct. COGS covers only costs tied to producing or acquiring the units sold. It excludes the store manager's salary, the rent, the electricity, the ad campaign — those existed whether or not that carton moved.

Use the same sorting test from module 03: if we sold one fewer unit, does this cost go down?

You'll notice COGS maps closely onto variable costs, and operating expenses onto fixed costs. Not identical in strict accounting terms, but close enough that the intuition carries in a case.

Start with $10 of revenue at a grocery chain and walk down. Each margin is that step's profit divided by the original $10.

Why this is a diagnostic tool, not trivia

The staircase tells you where in the business the bleeding is happening. Same "profits fell 12%," three completely different cases:

Gross margin fell

25% → 20%. The problem sits between you and your suppliers, or on your price tag. Suppliers raised prices, or you discounted, or your mix shifted to cheaper-margin goods. Rent and staff are irrelevant here.

Gross held, operating fell

5% → 2%. You're buying and selling exactly as profitably as before — overhead ballooned. New leases, more headcount, a marketing push that didn't pay back.

And if both held but net margin fell, you took on debt or a tax change hit. Rarely the interesting case, but worth ruling out in one line.

The single best clarifying question in profitability cases

"Is that 12% decline in gross, operating, or net profit?" It isn't pedantry — the answer eliminates roughly two-thirds of your issue tree before you've drawn it. Ask it in your first two questions, every time.

CHECKPOINT
  • Revenue flat, gross margin steady at 25%, operating profit down hard. Where do you look first, and why not at suppliers?
  • A company's profit in rupees rose while its net margin fell. What happened?
  • Why would a software firm and a grocery chain never share a margin benchmark?

PRODUCT MIX

Profit falls and nobody did anything wrong

This is the concept that catches out the most candidates, because it's invisible if you only look at averages.

Here's the setup. Every product a company sells has its own margin. Some are fat, some are thin. The mix is what proportion of your sales comes from each. Change the mix and your overall profitability changes — even if not a single product's price, cost, or margin moved.

Watch it happen

A retail chain sells groceries at a 30% margin. It adds electronics, which carry a 10% margin but much higher ticket prices. A year later:

 BeforeAfter
Grocery revenue$100 million$80 million
Electronics revenue$0$80 million
Grocery margin30%30%
Electronics margin—10%
Total revenue$100 million$160 million
Gross profit$30 million$32 million
Blended margin30%20%

Revenue up 60%. Margin collapsed by a third. And nobody made a mistake on any individual product — grocery still earns exactly what it always did, electronics is performing exactly as expected.

There's a second, nastier layer here. Notice grocery revenue fell from $100 million to $80 million Why? Because electronics took shelf space away from groceries. That's called cannibalisation — the new product didn't purely add, it partly replaced. Always ask: did the new revenue come on top of the old, or at the expense of it?

Say this in an interview

"Before I look at price or cost, I'd like to check whether our product mix has changed — specifically whether a larger share of revenue is now coming from lower-margin lines. And if so, whether that came in addition to existing revenue or at the expense of it."

That question, asked early, has cracked more profitability cases than any framework.

Sense checkMix is the trap you don't see coming, which is exactly why it catches people. Everything checks out strand by strand — every product performing to spec — and the structure still sags. When each part is fine but the whole isn't, look at the proportions between the parts.

What you actually do about it

The naive answer is "stop selling electronics." Usually wrong — electronics added $2 million of absolute gross profit. Remember module 05: margin percentage and absolute profit are different questions. The business got less efficient and more profitable.

Better recommendations look like:

  • Rebalance shelf space toward higher-margin lines, as long as total profit doesn't fall
  • Attach high-margin items to low-margin ones — sell the TV at 10%, sell the wall-mount, cable, and extended warranty at 60%
  • Check whether the low-margin line drives footfall that lifts other categories. Milk and eggs are deliberately sold at near-zero margin as loss leaders, because they pull people into a store where they buy profitable things
  • Ask whether the shift was expected. If management modelled a 3% margin dip and got 10%, the mix isn't the whole story — something else is also broken

Mix hides in more places than products

Mix effects also run through:

  • Channel mix — online orders may carry delivery costs that in-store ones don't
  • Customer mix — a shift toward bulk buyers who demand discounts
  • Geographic mix — growth concentrated in cities with higher rent and lower ticket sizes
  • Pack-size mix — a drink sold as 1L and 250ml bottles has different per-litre economics; if customers shift to small packs, per-litre revenue changes even at constant prices
CHECKPOINT
  • Explain to a friend how profit margin can fall while every product's margin stays identical.
  • What's the difference between "the new line cannibalised the old" and "the new line added revenue"? Why does it change your recommendation?
  • Name a loss leader you've personally bought this month.

PRICE ELASTICITY

Two forces fight. Elasticity tells you which one wins.

The question it answers

When you raise your price, two things happen simultaneously and they pull in opposite directions:

Good

You earn more on every unit you do sell.

Bad

Some customers walk away, so you sell fewer units.

Elasticity is simply the measurement of how hard customers react. It tells you which force wins.

ELASTICITY =
% CHANGE IN QUANTITY ÷ % CHANGE IN PRICE

Raise price 10%, volume falls 15% → elasticity = 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5.

(Technically it's negative, since price and quantity move opposite ways. Everyone drops the sign and talks in magnitudes. Don't be clever about this in an interview.)

Reading the number

ValueNameWhat it means
Greater than 1ElasticCustomers reacted more than you moved price. Sensitive. Volume loss beats the price gain.
Less than 1InelasticCustomers barely flinched. Price up 10%, volume down 4%. You just made money.
Exactly 1Unit elasticThe two forces cancel. Revenue unchanged.

See it in rupees

You sell 1,000 units at $10. Revenue = $10,000. You raise price 10%, to $11.

Elastic — elasticity 1.5

Volume falls 15% → 850 units.
Revenue = 850 × $11 = $9,350.
You lost $650. Bad move.

Inelastic — elasticity 0.4

Volume falls 4% → 960 units.
Revenue = 960 × $11 = $10,560.
You gained $560. Good move.

Same 10% increase. Opposite outcomes. The only variable is customer behaviour.

Sense checkThe same bottle of water is elastic in a supermarket and inelastic at an airport. Nothing about the bottle changed — the surroundings did. Elasticity is a property of the situation, never of the object, and candidates who forget this recommend price rises that only work in one of the two rooms.

And the profit picture in the inelastic case is even better than the revenue picture, because you're serving 40 fewer customers — less stock bought, less handling, lower variable cost. This is why price increases on inelastic goods are the cleanest profit lever that exists, and why "can we raise price?" should be one of your earliest questions in any profitability case.

What makes something elastic or inelastic

Don't memorise a list of goods. Ask three questions:

1 · Can they get it somewhere else easily? (substitutes)

Coca-Cola in a supermarket, with Pepsi on the next shelf — highly elastic. The identical Coca-Cola in a stadium with nothing else on sale — inelastic. Same product, opposite answer, because the substitutes changed. Elasticity is a property of the situation, not the object.

2 · Is it painful to switch? (switching costs)

Your bank, your phone number, your company's ERP system. Even when a cheaper option exists, the hassle of moving keeps you put. High switching costs make demand inelastic and let firms raise prices quietly — which is why so much of business strategy is about creating switching costs.

3 · Do they have to buy it? (necessity vs discretion)

Insulin, petrol for a commute, salt, electricity. Consumption barely moves with price. Contrast a restaurant meal or a cinema ticket — people simply skip those when money's tight.

One more: how big is it in their budget? A 20% rise on chewing gum is invisible. A 20% rise on a car is a family conference.

The traps that show up in real cases

Elasticity varies by segment. A grocery chain has loyal older shoppers who come regardless (inelastic) and price-hunting students who defect instantly (elastic). "The" elasticity is a blend of both. Segment before you conclude, or you'll recommend a price rise that keeps the low-value customers and loses the high-value ones.

Elasticity varies by product within one store. Milk and eggs are famously elastic because everyone knows what they should cost — that's exactly why they're sold at near-zero margin. The specialty cheese nobody can price-check is inelastic, and that's where margin lives. Notice this is a product mix insight arriving through the elasticity door — these concepts interlock.

It runs both directions. If demand is elastic, cutting price grows revenue — you gain more volume than you lose in margin. If it's inelastic, cutting price is self-harm: you gave up margin and got almost nothing back. A surprising number of real cases are about a client who discounted into a hole.

Cross-price elasticity. How your demand responds when a competitor moves price. If a rival cuts 10% and you lose 20% of volume, you're in a commodity knife-fight and pricing is not your lever — differentiation or cost is.

Say this in an interview

"Before recommending a price change I'd want to understand elasticity in this category — specifically whether customers have easy substitutes and low switching costs. If they do, a price increase will cost us more in volume than we gain in margin, and I'd look at cost structure instead."

CHECKPOINT
  • Price up 5%, volume down 2%. Elastic or inelastic? Should you do it?
  • Why is the same bottle of water elastic in a supermarket and inelastic at an airport?
  • Name three things your own household would keep buying at +30%, and three you'd drop.

THE FOUR WAYS TO PRICE

Floor, position, ceiling, and the fallback nobody mentions

The mental model first

A price sits inside a band. Below the floor you lose money on every sale. Above the ceiling nobody buys, because the thing isn't worth that much to them. Your actual price lives somewhere in between, and the four methods are four different ways of finding the edges and then choosing a point inside.

FLOOR ← COST-BASED | COMPETITOR-BASED | VALUE-BASED → CEILING
Ask this before any pricing maths

What is the client's pricing goal? Maximise profit? Grab market share fast? Recover R&D within three years? Deter a competitor from entering? The same product gets a wildly different price depending on the answer. Candidates who skip this and jump to arithmetic get punished for it.

1 · Cost-based — finds your floor

The logic: whatever we charge must at minimum cover what each unit costs us, plus a contribution toward fixed costs, plus a target margin.

The mechanics. Start with variable cost per unit — that's the absolute floor, since selling below it makes every extra sale worse. Then decide how fast to recover fixed costs.

Variable cost per unit$20
Investment $500,000, recovered over 100,000 units$5
Breakeven price$25
Target margin 20%$5
Price$30

Where you see it: industries with standardised products and little differentiation, where everyone applies a conventional margin over cost. This creates a genuinely useful diagnostic — if competitors sell at $10 and you're at $15 on the same margin convention, your costs are 50% higher. The pricing question just became a cost question.

Its weakness: entirely inward-looking. It has no idea whether customers value the product at $30 or $300. A pharma company pricing a life-saving drug on cost-plus would leave enormous money on the table. Use it as a floor check, never as the answer alone.

2 · Competitor-based — finds your market position

The logic: customers compare. Whatever you charge, they're mentally placing you next to the alternative.

The mechanics: benchmark the closest competing products, then adjust systematically for real differences.

  • Ours lasts twice as long → premium
  • Our after-sales service is worse → discount
  • Our brand is stronger → premium
  • Ours requires the customer to change their workflow → discount

Market leader charges $100. You perform 30% better on the metric customers care about, but your distribution is weaker so you're harder to buy. You might land at $115 — premium for performance, tempered by the access disadvantage.

Two weaknesses, and the second is what interviewers probe.

First, it anchors you to someone else's judgement. If the whole industry is underpricing because nobody did the value maths, you inherit their mistake.

Second, it invites price wars. If price is your only differentiator, the competitor matches, you both cut again, and margins collapse for everyone. When you use competitor-based pricing in a case, say explicitly: "and I'd want to check whether the competitor can and will match this move." That single sentence separates you from the field.

3 · Value-based — finds your ceiling

The logic: price against what the product is worth to the customer, not what it costs you.

Industrial example. A machine part reduces factory downtime by 4 hours a month. Downtime costs that factory $5,000 an hour. You've created $20,000 of monthly value. The customer won't hand over all of it — they need a reason to buy — so you capture perhaps 30–40%, pricing at $6,000–80,000 a month. Even though the part costs you $500 to make.

Consumer example. Cosmetics, luxury goods, education. Value is emotional or aspirational rather than a downtime calculation, so you triangulate — what do people already pay for comparable benefits in adjacent categories?

How to actually do it in an interview: ask "what does the customer currently do instead, and what does that cost them?" That's your reference point. Value-based pricing is almost always value relative to the next-best alternative, not value in the abstract.

Its weakness: value varies hugely by customer segment — which is exactly why price discrimination exists. A student and a corporation value the same software very differently, hence student pricing, enterprise tiers, and regional pricing.

4 · Elasticity-based — the fallback nobody mentions

Candidates almost never bring this up, and bringing it up is a genuine differentiator — provided you know when it applies. Use it when all three others break at once:

  • Cost-based fails because cost is trivial relative to value — a pill that costs $0.50 to make and saves a life
  • Competitor-based fails because there is no competitor — a patent, a first mover, a genuinely novel product
  • Value-based fails because the value is enormous and not meaningfully quantifiable — what exactly is the rupee value of surviving?

What's left is simply: what will people actually pay, and how many of them? Take the demand curve and pick the price that maximises (price − variable cost) × quantity.

PriceBuyersContribution/unitTotal profit
$5001,000,000$450$450 million
$1,000600,000$950$570 million
$2,000200,000$1,950$390 million

$1,000 wins. You didn't reason from cost, competitors, or abstract value — you reasoned from affordability across a population. Variable cost here is $50.

How to deliver this in an interview

The 25-second version

"There are four angles I'd use. Cost-based gives me a floor, competitor-based tells me market position, value-based gives me a ceiling, and elasticity-based if the first three don't constrain us. Here — since there's an established competitor set and a measurable performance advantage — I'd anchor on competitor-based and sanity-check against value to the customer. Cost is a floor check but shouldn't drive the answer. Can I ask what the closest competing products currently charge?"

Notice the structure: name all four, pick the relevant ones, justify why, then ask for the data you need. Breadth and judgement demonstrated simultaneously.

The hidden answer in pricing cases

Pricing cases frequently conceal a segmentation answer. If the honest response is "different customers value this very differently," the right recommendation may be multiple prices — tiers, regional pricing, bundles, student rates, peak/off-peak — rather than one number. Interviewers like this because it shows you resisted the pull of a single tidy answer.

CHECKPOINT
  • A new drug: no competitor, $0.50 production cost, life-saving. Which method, and why do the other three fail?
  • You recommend undercutting the market leader by 15%. What's the first question the interviewer will ask?
  • Give a value-based price for a service that saves a business 10 hours a week.

ECONOMIES OF SCALE

Why the big player wins, and when it stops being true

The core idea

Economies of scale means your cost per unit falls as you produce or sell more.

That's it. Make 100 shirts and each costs $40. Make 100,000 and each costs $24. Nothing about the shirt changed — the scale did.

This is why the phrase shows up in almost every growth, market entry, and competition case. It's the mechanical reason a large incumbent can price below a new entrant and still make money, and understanding which of the sources is at work turns a vague "they have scale" into an actual argument.

The five sources — know these individually

1 · Spreading fixed costs

The most important one, and it's just module 03 in reverse. A factory costs $10 million whether it makes 100,000 units or 1 million. Fixed cost per unit drops from $100 to $10 purely by producing more.

This is why high-fixed-cost industries — airlines, telecom, software, pharma, semiconductors — consolidate into a handful of giant players. The economics push relentlessly toward size.

2 · Purchasing power

Buy 1 million units of raw material and suppliers give you a better rate than they give the person buying 10,000. You're a bigger share of their order book and they'll fight to keep you. This lowers your variable cost, which is why it's so valuable — it improves contribution margin on every single unit.

Interview caution: this is not automatic. In a case with 40 stores, the honest answer to "can we use our combined bargaining power?" might be no — if suppliers are concentrated, or you're buying a commodity with thin supplier margins, or contracts are locked. Always ask rather than assume.

3 · Operational and technical efficiency

Bigger operations justify specialised equipment and dedicated roles. A small bakery has one person doing everything; a large one has a dedicated mixing line, a packaging line, and a logistics manager. Specialisation raises output per person.

There's a physical version too. Make a tank twice as wide and it holds eight times as much — but you don't need eight times the metal to build it. That's why ships, pipelines and chemical plants keep getting bigger.

4 · The learning curve

You get better at things by doing them repeatedly. The hundredth aircraft a factory builds takes far fewer hours than the tenth — fewer mistakes, better tools, a smoother process. What matters here is total units ever made, not units made this year. Which means a company that started earlier can stay cheaper for years, purely because of the head start.

5 · Marketing, distribution and capital

One national ad campaign costs the same whether you have 50 stores or 500 — cost per store plummets. A full truck costs little more than a half-empty one. And large firms borrow at lower interest rates because lenders see them as safer, lowering the cost of every rupee they invest.

Diseconomies of scale — the other half nobody mentions

Cost per unit does not fall forever. Past a point it turns and rises. Naming this in an interview is a strong signal, because it explains cases where a company grew fast and got less profitable.

Coordination cost

More people means more meetings, more approvals, more handoffs, slower decisions. Layers of management get added just to manage the layers below.

Motivation & distance

In a 10-person firm everyone sees their impact. In a 10,000-person one, individual effort gets diluted and accountability blurs.

Complexity cost

More SKUs, more locations, more systems. Inventory gets harder, IT gets tangled, the supply chain gets fragile.

Stretching the good stuff

Growth means the second-best locations, second-best staff, longer supply routes. The best options got used first.

Put together, cost per unit traces a U: falling through the efficient range, flat at the optimum, rising past it. Minimum efficient scale is the volume where you've captured most of the available economies. If MES is small relative to the market, many firms can compete efficiently and the industry stays fragmented — think restaurants. If MES is huge, only a few can reach it and you get an oligopoly — think aircraft manufacturing.

Sense checkBigger is not automatically stronger. A web spun too wide has strands too thin to hold anything, and a firm scaled past its optimum has exactly the same problem — reach without tension. When a client grew fast and got worse, this is your first hypothesis, and almost nobody in the room will name it.

Two things scale is often confused with

Economies of scope — cost advantages from producing a variety of things, not more of one thing. A dairy that makes milk, curd, cheese and butter shares the same collection network, refrigeration and distribution. Neither product alone would justify that infrastructure. This is the underlying logic of most conglomerate and product-extension arguments.

Network effects — the product becomes more valuable to each user as more people use it, which is a demand-side effect. Scale is supply-side: it makes things cheaper to produce. A telephone network has both. A cement plant has only scale. Don't use the words interchangeably; interviewers hear the difference.

Say this in an interview

"The competitor's cost advantage may be structural rather than operational. At three times our volume they'd be spreading the same fixed base over more units and likely getting better input pricing. I'd want to compare fixed cost per unit and variable cost per unit separately — if the gap is in fixed cost per unit, the answer is volume, not cost-cutting."

INDUSTRY STRUCTURE

Why some industries are just harder to make money in

Everything so far has been about a single company's numbers. This module zooms out to the industry, and answers a question those numbers can't: why are margins in this business what they are in the first place?

The tool is called Porter's Five Forces. Don't recite it — use it to explain where the money goes. Picture the industry's total profit as a pot. Five different parties are all pulling at that pot. The harder any one of them pulls, the less is left for the companies inside the industry.

Rivalry among existing players

How hard the current competitors fight. Rises with: many similar-sized competitors, low product differentiation, slow market growth (the only way to grow is to steal share), high fixed costs (everyone desperate for volume), and high exit barriers (nobody can afford to leave).

Symptom in a case: constant discounting, promotions, price matching. Margins thin for everyone, not just your client.

Threat of new entrants

How easily someone new can show up. Falls with: high capital requirements, regulatory licensing, strong incumbent brands, proprietary technology, locked-up distribution, and — connecting to module 09 — high minimum efficient scale.

Symptom: your client is profitable today, but three funded startups appeared last year. Today's margin isn't tomorrow's.

Threat of substitutes

Not competitors selling the same thing — different things solving the same need. Video calls substitute for business flights. Tea substitutes for coffee. Rises with low switching costs and decent substitute performance.

Symptom: the category itself is shrinking while your market share holds. That's a substitute problem, and no amount of competing harder within the category fixes it.

Bargaining power of buyers

Rises when: buyers are few and large, they're price sensitive, the product is undifferentiated, switching costs are low, they can easily compare prices, or they could plausibly make it themselves.

Symptom: a supplier whose top three customers are 70% of revenue has almost no pricing freedom — those customers dictate terms.

Bargaining power of suppliers

Rises when: few suppliers exist, their input is differentiated or critical, switching suppliers is costly, or they could move downstream and become your competitor.

Symptom: input costs rise and you can't pass them on. That's supplier power on one side and buyer power on the other, squeezing from both ends.

Translating forces into margins

The sentence that makes this useful

High rivalry + high buyer power + low switching costs = structurally thin margins, and no amount of clever strategy fixes that. Conversely, high barriers to entry + differentiated product + high switching costs = fat, defensible margins.

Saying this tells an interviewer you understand industries, not just frameworks.

This matters enormously for recommendations. If a client's margins are thin because the industry is structurally brutal, telling them to "improve operational efficiency" is treating a symptom. The real options are to change position — differentiate, move up the value chain, build switching costs, consolidate the industry — or accept the margins and compete on volume and cost leadership.

Two companion tools worth knowing

The value chain. The sequence of steps from raw input to the customer: R&D → sourcing → manufacturing → warehousing and transport → distribution → retail and marketing → after-sales. Its use in cases is as an escape hatch: when the profit tree tells you "volume fell" but not why, walk the chain. Is it a production issue (we can't make enough), a distribution push issue (retailers aren't stocking or pushing us — worse margins than competitors offer them, poor shelf placement, awkward packaging), or a customer pull issue (people simply want less of it)? Those three cover the entire space, they don't overlap, and each leads somewhere concrete.

PESTEL — Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal. A checklist for external factors when a case involves something outside the industry: a regulation change, a currency move, a demographic shift. Use it as a mental sweep, not as a structure to present.

CHECKPOINT
  • Pick an industry you know. Rate all five forces high/medium/low and predict its margins.
  • Volume fell 15%. Walk the value chain and give one testable cause at each of the three stages.
  • Why is "improve efficiency" often the wrong answer in a structurally low-margin industry?

HOW TO ESTIMATE ANYTHING

Guessing a number properly, with no data at all

Interviewers ask things like "how many cups of coffee are sold in Chicago each day?" or "how big is the market for gym memberships in the US?"

You obviously don't know. They know you don't know. They're not testing your knowledge — they're watching whether you can break an impossible question into small pieces you can reason about, say your assumptions out loud, and do arithmetic without panicking.

Being 40% off with clean logic beats being 5% off with a lucky guess. Every time.

The method, in four steps

  1. Say what you're actually counting. "Cups of coffee" — does that include home-made? Restaurants? Just street carts? Pick one, say it out loud, move on.
  2. Break it into a multiplication chain. Keep splitting until you reach numbers a normal person could estimate.
  3. Fill in each piece with a number, and say why you chose it.
  4. Multiply, then sanity-check the answer against something you know.

Two directions to come at it

Top-down — start big, cut down

Begin with a population and narrow with percentages.

"340 million people → 83% urban → of those, 60% drink coffee → 2 cups a day…"

Best for consumer markets where population is the natural starting point.

Bottom-up — start small, scale up

Begin with one unit and multiply outward.

"One coffee shop sells 300 cups a day → roughly 20,000 shops in the city…"

Best when you can picture one unit clearly — a shop, a store, a machine.

Which to pick? Whichever gives you numbers you're more confident guessing. And if you have time, do both and see if they roughly agree — that's a genuinely impressive move, because two independent methods landing near each other is real evidence.

A full worked example

How many cups of coffee are sold by shops and cafés in Chicago each day?

First, scope it out loud: "I'll count coffee bought from shops, cafés and carts — not coffee made at home."

Top-down

StepReasoningNumber
Chicago metro populationRoughly 9 million9,000,000
Coffee drinkersSay 70% drink coffee regularly6,300,000
Who buy it outsideSay half buy at least some outside home — commuters, office workers3,150,000
Cups bought outside per dayAverage 1.5 — some buy one, many buy two× 1.5
Daily cups ≈ 4.7 million

Bottom-up, as a cross-check

StepReasoningNumber
Coffee shops in ChicagoShops, cafés, carts — say 1 per 500 people18,000
Cups per shop per dayA busy shop does ~300; many are smaller. Average 250× 250
Daily cups = 4.5 million

4.7 million versus 4.5 million. Two completely different routes, near-identical answers. That's a strong signal your assumptions are reasonable, and saying so out loud is worth real credit.

Want it in dollars? Multiply by price: 4.5 million cups × $1.50 = $6.75 million a day, roughly $2.4 billion a year.

Sanity-checking — the step everyone skips

Before you announce a number, hold it up against something you already know:

  • Per person, does it make sense? 4.5 million cups ÷ 9 million people = one cup every two days per Chicagoan. Plausible.
  • Is it absurdly large or small? If your estimate for US car sales came out at 500 million a year, something's badly wrong — that's more cars than households.
  • Does it beat a number you do know? If your city's market is bigger than the national market, you've made an error somewhere.

If it fails the check, say so and fix it. Catching your own error is a positive signal, not a negative one. Hiding it is the only real failure.

Useful numbers to have in your head

You don't need many. These cover most estimates:

ThingRough number
US population~340 million

Average household size (US)~2.5 people
Big metro population3–9 million
World population~8 billion
Days in a year365, use 350 or 300 for shops
Working hours in a day8–10

And a habit that saves you: round aggressively. Use 9 million, not 9.4 million. Nobody wants to watch you do long division, and the precision is fake anyway.

Arithmetic that doesn't fall apart under pressure

  • Work in powers of ten. 3.4 × 10⁸ × 0.83 is easier to track than 340,000,000 × 83%
  • Multiply the easy pairs first to keep numbers round
  • Say your numbers out loud as you go. If you make a mistake silently the interviewer can't help you; if you say it, they usually will
  • Write neatly. One assumption per line, labelled. If you have to backtrack, you can
How to open a sizing question

"Let me scope this first — I'll estimate coffee bought from shops and cafés in Chicago, not home-made. I'll work top-down from population, and if there's time I'd like to cross-check bottom-up from the number of coffee shops. Starting with a Chicago metro population of about 9 million…"

Scope, method, cross-check, first number. Fifteen seconds and the interviewer already knows you've done this before.

  • Do the same question the other direction — if you went top-down, now go bottom-up. Do they agree?
  • Your estimate for gas stations in your city comes to 40,000. Sanity-check it. What's wrong?

TAM · SAM · SOM

From "the market is huge" to a number you can defend

The problem these three words solve

A weak candidate sizes a market and says "the US shampoo market is $30 billion, so this is a big opportunity." That's nearly useless. Nobody sells to all of the US. You sell through specific channels, in specific regions, to specific people who'd actually buy your specific product — and you fight everyone else for that slice.

TAM, SAM and SOM are the discipline of narrowing from "the whole world could theoretically buy this" down to "here's what we'll actually book next year." Three concentric circles, each smaller and more honest.

TAM — Total Addressable Market

Everyone who could conceivably buy this category, if all constraints vanished. All geographies, all channels, all competitors, 100% share. It's the ceiling, and it answers exactly one question: is this category big enough to be worth entering at all?

If TAM is $200 million, no amount of brilliant execution makes this a large business. If TAM is $50 billion, keep going — but TAM alone says nothing about whether you can win.

SAM — Serviceable Addressable Market

The part of TAM you could realistically reach given your structural constraints:

  • Geography — you operate in 3 states, not 28
  • Channel — you sell online; most category sales happen in kirana stores you can't reach
  • Segment — your product is premium; lower income tiers won't buy at any share
  • Regulation — you're licensed in some markets and not others

SAM still assumes you capture all of it. It's the size of your pond, not your catch.

SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market

What you'll actually capture near-term, because competitors exist. This is where you apply a market share assumption — and where you must justify the number rather than pluck it from air. Look at what a comparable new entrant achieved. Check whether the market is fragmented or concentrated. Ask whether you have a genuine differentiator or just another option.

The whole thing, worked

Client makes premium organic protein bars and wants to enter the US.

StepWorkingResult
TAM340 million people × 15% who buy packaged bars × 20 bars/yr × $5$5.1 billion
Narrow: metros onlyMetros ≈ 30% of premium snack spend× 30%
Narrow: premium tier≈ 40% of metro spend is premium-capable× 40%
Narrow: our channelsModern retail + e-comm ≈ 70% of premium metro sales× 70%
SAMThe honest pond$428 million
Market share3 incumbents hold 60%; funded entrant with real differentiation× 4%
SOMWhat goes in the business case≈ $17 million

$5.1 billion → $60 million Same market. Those two numbers support completely opposite decisions — if the client needs $200 million of revenue to justify the investment, the answer is no, and you would never have found that by stopping at TAM.

Why interviewers care

It shows you separate opportunity from capture. Plenty of candidates size a market, announce it's huge, and recommend entry. The follow-up — "and how much of that do you get?" — ends them.

Every narrowing step is a testable assumption. When you say "we keep 30% because we're metro-only," the interviewer can push: "actually we'd launch in tier-2 too." Now you adjust one number instead of rebuilding the estimate. That's what clean structure buys you.

It feeds straight into the profit equation. The market entry formula is:

PROFIT = MARKET SIZE × MARKET SHARE
× (PRICE − VARIABLE COST) − FIXED COSTS

Market size × market share is SOM. So this vocabulary isn't decoration — it's the front end of the calculation that decides the case. And notice the back half is module 01. Everything connects.

Two traps

Don't over-narrow to nothing. Some candidates slice so hard that SOM becomes trivially small and the recommendation is automatically "don't enter." Every cut needs a stated reason, and you should sanity-check the final number against reality — if a comparable player already does $300 million and your SOM says $5 million, an assumption is wrong.

Don't mix SAM and SOM constraints. Geography and channel are structural → SAM. Competition and brand weakness are competitive → SOM. Mixing them double-counts and understates.

Say this in an interview

"I'll size this in three steps. First the total addressable market for the category, to check it's big enough to matter. Then I'll narrow to what we can serve given our geography and channel constraints. Then I'll apply a market share assumption based on the competitive landscape to reach a realistic obtainable figure — and that's the number I'd feed into the profit calculation."

One sentence of setup that tells the interviewer the entire arc of your next four minutes.

CHECKPOINT
  • Size the TAM for coffee shops in a city of 2 million people. Show your assumptions.
  • Which of these belong in SAM and which in SOM: weak brand, no licence in 2 states, 4 strong incumbents, offline-only distribution?
  • Your SOM comes out at $4 million and an existing rival does $250 million What do you do?

UNIT ECONOMICS

The metrics that decide whether a modern business is real

Student consulting clubs work with startups constantly, so these turn up more than classical frameworks do. The underlying question is always the same: does this business make more from a customer than it spent to get them?

Unit economics is just the profit equation zoomed all the way in — to one customer instead of the whole company.

CAC — Customer Acquisition Cost

CAC = TOTAL SALES & MARKETING SPEND
÷ NEW CUSTOMERS ACQUIRED

Spend $100,000 on marketing in a month, get 2,000 new customers → CAC = $50.

What to probe: CAC almost always rises as a company scales. The cheapest, most obvious customers get acquired first; reaching the next million requires more expensive channels. A startup showing flat CAC projections over three years is showing you a fantasy — and saying that in an interview is a strong moment.

Also check whether CAC is blended (all spend ÷ all new customers, including ones who'd have come anyway) or paid (paid spend ÷ customers from paid channels). Blended CAC flatters the numbers.

LTV — Lifetime Value

LTV = AVERAGE REVENUE PER CUSTOMER PER PERIOD
× CONTRIBUTION MARGIN %
× AVERAGE LIFETIME IN PERIODS

A subscriber pays $40 a month, contribution margin is 60%, and the average customer stays 20 months → LTV = 40 × 0.60 × 20 = $480.

The single most common beginner error: using revenue instead of contribution. A customer paying $40 a month isn't giving you $40 — after the cost of serving them you might keep $24. LTV built on revenue overstates the business, sometimes by 3×. Always multiply by margin.

The ratio that decides everything

LTV ÷ CAC
RatioReading
Below 1Every new customer destroys value. Growth makes it worse, not better.
1 to 3Working but thin. Little room for fixed costs on top.
Around 3The conventional health benchmark.
Well above 3Healthy — but possibly underinvesting in growth. Could spend more to acquire faster.

Note the direction of that last row. A very high ratio isn't automatically the best answer; it can mean the company is leaving growth on the table.

Payback period

PAYBACK = CAC ÷ MONTHLY CONTRIBUTION PER CUSTOMER

CAC $50, monthly contribution $24 → about 2.1 months to recover the acquisition cost.

This matters as much as LTV/CAC and gets mentioned far less. A business with a great ratio but a 30-month payback needs enormous cash to fund growth — it's paying upfront and collecting slowly. Payback measures cash risk; LTV/CAC measures profitability. Both, or you're only half informed.

Churn — the quiet killer

MONTHLY CHURN = CUSTOMERS LOST IN MONTH
÷ CUSTOMERS AT START OF MONTH

The link people miss: average customer lifetime ≈ 1 ÷ churn rate. At 5% monthly churn, customers stay about 20 months. At 10%, only 10 months — which halves your LTV. Churn doesn't just lose customers, it silently rewrites your entire unit economics.

When a case says "customers keep cancelling after month two," that's a churn case, and the branches are:

  • Wrong customers acquired — the marketing promised something the product doesn't do
  • Onboarding failure — they never reached the moment where the product became useful
  • Value gap — the product genuinely isn't worth the price after novelty fades
  • Competitive pull — someone else offered better, and switching was easy

Also distinguish voluntary churn (they chose to leave) from involuntary churn (their card expired and nobody chased it). The second is often 20–40% of total churn and is far cheaper to fix.

AOV — Average Order Value

AOV = TOTAL REVENUE ÷ NUMBER OF ORDERS

Why it's a lever: in a delivery business, much of the cost per order is fixed per order — the rider, the packaging, the pick-and-pack. Raising AOV from $30 to $45 spreads that cost over more revenue and can flip contribution margin positive without acquiring a single new customer. Hence minimum order values, bundles, free-delivery thresholds, and "frequently bought together."

GMV — Gross Merchandise Value

GMV = NUMBER OF TRANSACTIONS × AOV

The total value of everything transacted on a platform. Critically, GMV is not revenue. A marketplace that processes $1 billion of GMV at a 15% take rate earns $150 million of revenue. Marketplaces love quoting GMV because it's the biggest available number.

Take rate = revenue ÷ GMV — the commission the platform keeps. When a case involves a marketplace, get the take rate early; without it, GMV tells you nothing about the business's actual size.

How they interlock

If this moves……this happens
Churn falls 10% → 5%Lifetime doubles, LTV doubles, LTV/CAC doubles
AOV risesContribution per order rises → LTV rises, payback shortens
CAC rises with scaleLTV/CAC falls, payback lengthens — growth gets expensive
Contribution margin improvesLTV rises and payback shortens simultaneously

Which is why in a startup case, the highest-leverage question is usually about churn or contribution margin — they each move two metrics at once.

Sense checkChurn is the strand you never notice snapping, because the structure holds right up until it doesn't. At 5% monthly churn customers stay 20 months; at 10% they stay 10, and your LTV silently halves while every dashboard still looks green. Check the anchor points before you admire the web.

Say this in an interview

"I'd start with unit economics before anything else. CAC is $50 and LTV is $480, so the ratio is roughly 9.6 — healthy. But payback is what worries me: if it's over a year, growth burns cash faster than the business generates it. And I'd want to know whether that CAC is blended or paid, because blended CAC tends to flatter the number."

CHECKPOINT
  • CAC $80, revenue $50/month, contribution margin 40%, monthly churn 8%. Find LTV, LTV/CAC and payback. Is this business viable?
  • Why is LTV calculated on revenue rather than contribution a dangerous mistake?
  • A marketplace reports $500 million GMV. What's the one question you must ask before reacting?

USING IT IN THE ROOM

Where each concept actually fires

Knowing eleven concepts is worth nothing if you can't reach for the right one under pressure. This is the retrieval map.

When you hear…Reach for
"I have no idea where to start"The profitability tree (02) — split money in vs money out and eliminate
"How many X are sold in Y?"Estimation method (11) — scope, chain, assume, sanity-check
"Profits have declined X%"Profit equation (01) → walk the tree (02) → ask gross/operating/net (05)
"Revenue grew but profit didn't"Product mix (06), then cost structure (03)
"Should we launch / enter this market?"Size it (11) → TAM·SAM·SOM (12) → profit equation (01) → industry structure (10)
"What should we charge?"Four pricing methods (08), sanity-checked against elasticity (07)
"Should we cut price to win share?"Elasticity (07) + rivalry and retaliation (10)
"How many units do we need to sell?"Breakeven (04)
"A competitor undercuts us and still profits"Economies of scale (09) — compare fixed and variable separately
"Customers keep cancelling"Churn and unit economics (13)
"The whole industry has thin margins"Five forces (10) — the problem may be structural
"Volume fell but we don't know why"The escape hatch (02): can we make it / can we get it out there / do people want it

The three habits that carry all of it

Compare against two things, always. How did this look for us before? How does it look for competitors? A number with no comparison is not a finding.

Quantify wherever you can. "Costs seem high" is worthless. "Variable cost per unit is $4 against a competitor's $2.80, and at our volume that's $6 million a year" is a recommendation waiting to happen.

Mini-synthesise after every branch. One line before you move on: "So distribution isn't the issue — retailer margins are above market. Let me check demand-side factors." This is what makes an interview sound like a conversation instead of a checklist being read aloud.

The last thing worth remembering

None of this is about having the right answer. Two candidates can reach opposite conclusions and the one with cleaner structure and better-defended numbers wins. These concepts exist so that when someone hands you an unfamiliar business problem, you always know where to look next. That's the whole skill.
