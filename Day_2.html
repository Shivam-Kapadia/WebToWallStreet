Day 1 in one page

Everything from yesterday, before you add anything new

Do not skip this bit. Everything in the next eleven sections sits on top of Day 1. If the base is shaky, the whole thing falls apart around minute four of a real case — and it falls apart in a way the interviewer can see clearly.

Read it once, slowly. Say the scripted lines out loud.

The four stages of any case

StageTimeWhat you do
Clarify2–3 minSay the problem back. Check what the client wants. Ask a few questions to fix the edges of the problem.
Structure2–3 minGo quiet for a minute. Draw a tree. Then say it out loud, big pieces first.
Analyse15–20 minGo down one branch at a time. Do the sums. Say what each number means.
Conclude1–2 minAnswer first. Then three reasons. Then one risk. Then one next step.
SAY IT LIKE THIS

Starting. “Let me check I have this right. The client is X, and they want Y. Before I structure this, can I ask a few questions?”

Buying yourself quiet time. “Could I take a minute to organise my thoughts?”

Giving your structure. “I want to split this into three parts. First… second… third… I would like to start with the first one, because I think the biggest driver sits there.”

Ending. “My recommendation is X. Three reasons… The main risk is… and the first thing I would do next is…”

MECE

It stands for mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. In plain words: no overlaps, no gaps. Each thing goes in exactly one box, and the boxes cover everything.

Here is the only test you need. Look at your boxes and ask: could a real cause of this problem be something that does not fit in any of my boxes? If yes, you have a gap. Add a box, or add “other” and say why you are leaving it aside for now.

NO OVERLAPS, NO GAPS

Money in / money out

US / outside the US

Customers we already have / new customers

Under 25 / 25 to 40 / over 40

BROKEN

Online / mobile / shops — a phone order is both online and mobile. One sale lands in two boxes.

Marketing problems / pricing problems — pricing is part of marketing, so they overlap. And nothing here covers the factory.

Clarifying questions vs guesses

This is the mistake you already got caught on once. It is worth going over again, because it is the quickest way to look like a beginner and also the quickest thing to fix.

A clarifying question asks where the edges of the problem are. A guess (a hypothesis) says what the cause might be. Both are useful. They just belong in different stages.

Clarifying question — stage 1Guess — stage 3
Is this happening in one region or everywhere?Could a local rival be cutting prices?
Which product are we talking about?Is the expensive range pulling the average down?
Over what time period did this happen?Did this start when the new factory opened?

Nothing in the right column is wrong. It is just early. Guessing at causes before you have drawn a structure looks like fishing. The exact same sentence, said twenty minutes later, looks smart.

The three things to ask about

  • What they want — what does success look like, what number are they aiming at, by when, and what can they not change?
  • Who they are — what do they sell, where, and are they the biggest player or a small one?
  • How wide the problem is — the whole company or one part, which product, what time period, and is this just them or the whole industry?

The profit tree

PROFIT

ASK THIS FIRST, EVERY TIME

Is it just our client, or the whole industry?

If the whole industry is struggling, look outside: the market shrank, rules changed, raw material got dearer, customers moved to something else. If it is only our client, look inside: we lost share, our mix changed, our costs went up.

One question, and you have cut the tree in half before spending any time on it.

When the tree gets stuck

Sometimes the tree tells you “we are selling fewer units” and then refuses to tell you more. Stop pushing. Turn the problem sideways and walk through how the product actually reaches a customer:

DESIGN → BUY MATERIALS → MAKE IT → STORE & MOVE IT → SELL IT → ADVERTISE IT → SUPPORT IT at each step ask: what could go wrong here?

The example worth remembering. A sweet company’s sales drop. The tree says “fewer units” and stalls. Walking the steps finds it: the distributor had switched to jars with narrow necks, so shopkeepers could not get their hand in easily and stopped offering them. No amount of thinking about price or rivals would ever have found that.

When the problem is “fewer customers”

Find the step where people drop out:

HEAR ABOUT US → CONSIDER US → BUY → COME BACK → TELL FRIENDS the step where the drop happens is your answer

The money maths

PROFIT = (PRICE − COST PER UNIT) × UNITS SOLD − FIXED COSTS every number you build today lives inside this line
  • Contribution margin = price minus the cost of making one more unit. It is what each sale puts towards paying the rent.
  • Breakeven volume = fixed costs divided by contribution margin. How many units before you stop losing money. Learn this one properly — section 03 is built on it.
  • The three margins. Gross margin is left after making the product. Operating margin is left after running the business. Net margin is what is left after interest and tax.
  • Profit is not profitability. Profit is a rupee amount. Profitability is profit as a share of sales. Sales can go up while profitability goes down.
  • Which products sell matters. Total sales can look flat while profit falls, because people switched from a $70-margin item to a $20-margin item. Always check this before saying nothing changed.
  • Price sensitivity. If people are very sensitive, cutting the price brings in more total money. If they are not sensitive, raising the price brings in more. Things people must have — medicine, fuel, salt — are not sensitive.
  • Bigger can be cheaper. Fixed costs spread across more units means each unit costs less. Only up to a point — past a certain size, managing the mess costs more than you save.

Guessing the size of a market

  • Top down — start with a big number and filter it: everyone in the US → only cities → only the right age → only those who can afford it.
  • Bottom up — start with one small thing and multiply: sales in one shop × number of shops.
  • From the buyer side — how much do people want to buy?
  • From the seller side — how much can anyone actually make or supply? Use this when supply is the limit: hospital beds, aeroplane seats, cement.

Worked example — dating app users in the US. Start with 1,400 million people. About 40% live in cities, so 560 million. Of those, roughly 105 million are aged 18 to 30. About 75% have internet, so around 79 million. Say a third of them would use a dating app. That gives about 28 million users.

Say each assumption out loud as you make it. Use round numbers you can hold in your head. Ask permission before rounding.

01

The one shape

Six case types, three questions

Yesterday you learned the profit tree. That tree solves one kind of case: something is broken, go and find it. Profits fell. Why? You narrow down until you hit the cause.

Now meet the other kind. Entering a new market, launching a product, setting a price, growing faster, buying a company, cutting costs — in all of these, nothing is broken. The client is standing at a fork in the road and wants to know which way to walk.

Beginners try to memorise six separate frameworks. Do not do that. They are all the same three questions, wearing different clothes.

The questionWhat you actually do
1Is it worth it?Work out a number. Compare it to what the client said they wanted.
2Can we actually do it?Walk through every step of making and selling the thing. Find the step that breaks.
3How should we do it?Pick the way in. Build it ourselves, buy someone, partner up, or pay someone else to do it.

If you remember nothing else today, remember that table. It means you never have to recall six frameworks. You recall one shape and change what goes inside it.

The version that loses marks

Most people answer a market entry case by reciting four boxes — customers, competition, product, company — then asking twenty scattered questions and hoping something useful turns up.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

“Who are the customers? What segments are there? Who are the competitors? What is their share? Are there barriers? What is our cost structure?”

Every question is fair. But they never add up to anything. After twenty minutes you have twenty facts and still have to wave your hands and say “so it looks attractive”.

The interviewer cannot tell if you are thinking or just remembering a list.

WHAT TO DO INSTEAD

Say up front: “I want to answer two questions. First, does this make money? Second, can we actually pull it off?”

Then every question you ask has a job. It feeds one of the numbers you are building.

By minute eight you have a rupee figure on the table, and everything after that is a real argument about a real number.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

“I would like to break this into two questions. First, does entering make money — I will build that up from the size of the market, the share we could win, and the profit per unit, against what we have to spend up front. Second, if the money works, can we actually run it — I will walk through making, moving and selling the product. If both look good, I will finish by suggesting how we should enter. Does that sound sensible?”

Sense check

Think of a decision case as deciding whether to throw a rope across a gap. Is it worth it asks whether the other side is worth reaching. Can we do it asks whether the rope will hold. How asks where to aim it. Skip the first and you build a lovely bridge to nowhere. Skip the second and you step out into air.

CHECKPOINT 01
  1. Say the difference between a “something is broken” case and a “should we do this” case in one sentence each.
  2. Name the three questions in order, without looking.
  3. Explain why listing four boxes of questions does not lead anywhere.
  4. Say the opening script above out loud in under thirty seconds.

02

Which one do I use?

Hearing the prompt and picking the right tool

Here is a gap most study guides leave open. You now know several frameworks. But in a real interview nobody tells you which one to use. You get one sentence, and you have about five seconds to work out what kind of case you are in.

So learn the triggers. Listen for what the client wants, not what industry they are in.

If the prompt sounds like…It is a…Go to
“Profits have fallen” · “Margins are down” · “We are losing money”Something is brokenProfit tree — section 00
“Should we enter the US?” · “We want to expand abroad”New marketSection 03
“We have invented something” · “Should we launch this?”New productSection 04
“What should we charge?” · “How do we price this?”PricingSection 05
“We want to double in three years” · “Growth has stalled”GrowthSection 06
“Should we buy this company?” · “Is this a good investment?”Buying a companySection 07
“We need to cut costs by 15%” · “Our factory is too expensive”Cost cuttingSection 08
“How many gas stations are there in Chicago?”Size guessSection 00

Most real cases are two cases stuck together

This trips people up. A market entry case almost always contains a size guess. A growth case often turns into a buying-a-company case halfway through. A pricing question can appear inside anything.

That is fine. It is not a trick. You just switch tools when the question changes, and you say so out loud so the interviewer can follow you.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

“To work out the money side, I first need the size of the market. Let me estimate that, and then come back to the entry decision.”

“We have shown that growing on our own gets us to 20%, not 30%. So the gap has to come from buying someone. Can I switch to looking at that?”

When you genuinely cannot tell

Ask. It costs you nothing and it is what a real consultant would do.

DO NOT GUESS SILENTLY

Picking the wrong framework and pushing on for fifteen minutes is much worse than asking one question at the start. Interviewers do not mark you down for asking what the client wants. They mark you down for building a careful answer to a question nobody asked.

Safe line: “Before I structure this, can I check what the client is trying to decide? Are they choosing whether to do this at all, or have they decided and want to know how?”

The one habit that saves you

Whatever the case type, write the client’s goal at the top of your page in their own words. Break even by year three. Get to 30% growth. Cut $50 million of cost.

Then every five minutes, glance at it. If what you are doing right now does not move towards that sentence, you have drifted. Come back.

CHECKPOINT 02
  1. Cover the table. Say what case type each of these is: “we want to grow 25% a year”, “our margins slipped last quarter”, “a rival is for sale”.
  2. Say the line you would use to switch from one framework to another mid-case.
  3. Write the goal sentence you would put at the top of your page for a client wanting to break even in the US by year three.

03

Entering a new market

Get to a number, then check the plan can run

The client sells something in one country and wonders about another. Or they are in one city and thinking about ten more. Same case either way.

What to ask before you structure

  • Why this market, and why now?
  • How will they judge success — total profit, breaking even by a certain year, market share, or just getting a foot in the door?
  • Same product, or changed for the new market?
  • Which parts will they run themselves, and which will they buy from others?
  • Have they tried entering somewhere before? What happened?

The structure

SHOULD WE ENTER?
DOES IT MAKE MONEY?
CAN WE ACTUALLY RUN IT?
Size of market
Share we can win
Profit per unit
and what we spend up front
Make it · move it · sell it
have it, build it, or buy it?
PROFIT = SIZE × SHARE × (PRICE − COST PER UNIT) − FIXED COSTS every question you ask should feed one of these five things

Worked example — a skincare brand comes to the US

An American company sells premium face cream made from natural ingredients. They want to enter the US. Two big American companies already own the market, both using chemical ingredients.

What we knowNumberWhere that gets us
Size of the market20 million jars a year
Share we hope for in year 15%1 million jars
Price per jar$150
Cost to make one jar$80So each jar earns $70
Money from all jars1,000,000 × $70$70 million
Factory and launch spend$200 million
Year 1 profit70 − 200Loss of $130 million

The move that actually scores

A weak answer stops here: “it loses money, so do not enter”. That is not consulting, that is arithmetic.

A strong answer flips the sum around. Instead of asking “what do we earn at 5% share?”, ask “what share would we need to stop losing money?”

JARS NEEDED = $200 million ÷ $70 = 2.86 million JARS
SHARE NEEDED = 2.86 ÷ 20 = 14.3% the loss was never the real question
SAY IT LIKE THIS

“So this is not really a question about a $130 million loss. It is a question about whether a foreign brand can take 14% of the market from two well-established players, and how many years that would take. How long is the client willing to fund losses for?”

You turned a number into a business question. That sentence is the whole difference between someone who can do sums and someone who can advise.

How long until we break even?

The interviewer will often push here, and many people freeze. It is simple. If we get to 5% share in year 1 and grow a few points each year, count how many years until share passes 14.3%. If the answer is seven years and the client wanted three, the answer is no — even though the plan works eventually.

TWO WORDS WORTH KNOWING

Payback period. How many years until the money we get back adds up to the money we put in. Short is good.

NPV. Money in five years is worth less than money today, because you could have earned interest on it. NPV shrinks future money down to today’s value and then checks whether the total beats what you spent. Positive means go ahead.

You will not be asked to build either one properly in an interview. You will be asked whether you know which one answers the question in front of you.

04

Launching a product

And the question almost nobody asks

This looks like market entry, but one thing is different. In market entry you are asking whether a place is worth going to. Here the market already exists. The question is narrower: will this particular product sell, at what price, to whom?

The structure

SHOULD WE LAUNCH IT?
Does anyone want it?
real need, or a gap nobody fills
Who pays for it?
our cash, a loan, or investors
Can we make and sell it?
materials, factory, shops, ads
Does it make money?
and when does it break even

The money question matters more than people expect. If the launch is paid for with our own cash, we can be patient. If it is paid for by investors who want a return in three years, the bar is much higher. Ask which it is.

The question almost nobody asks

ARE WE STEALING FROM OURSELVES?

If the client already sells something similar, some people buying the new product would have bought the old one anyway. That sale is not new money. You have just moved it from one shelf to another.

This has an ugly name — cannibalisation — but the idea is simple: only count sales that would not have happened otherwise.

Ask it plainly: “Do we expect some of these sales to come out of our existing product? Roughly what share?”

Worked example — a biscuit company launches a protein bar

A US biscuit maker wants to launch a protein bar. Here is the sum, done twice — once the way most people do it, once properly.

StepWorkingResult
Bars we expect to sell in year 1—500,000
Price $6, cost to make $3.5060 − 35$2.50 per bar
Money from bars500,000 × $2.50$1.25 million
Setting up the line and advertising—$800,000
The easy answer1.25 − 0.80Profit $450,000 — launch it

Now add the question nobody asks. Suppose 30% of protein bar buyers would otherwise have bought our biscuit pack, which earns us $1 each.

StepWorkingResult
Bars that steal a biscuit sale30% of 500,000150,000
Biscuit money we lose150,000 × $1$150,000
Real new money12.5 million − 1.5 million$1.1 million
The honest answer1.10 − 0.80Profit $300,000
WHY THIS IS WORTH DOING

Here the launch still works, just less well. But push the numbers a little — make it 60% stealing, or make the biscuit margin $2 instead of $1 — and the whole launch turns into a loss.

That is the point. A product can look profitable and still make the company poorer. Very few candidates check this. Checking it makes you look like you have actually run a business.

One more thing to raise

Ask what rivals will do. If we launch a protein bar and it works, how long before the two big players launch theirs? If the answer is four months, our year-2 numbers are far too optimistic. Saying this out loud costs ten seconds and shows you are thinking past the spreadsheet.

  2. Explain in one sentence why stealing sales from your own product matters.
  3. Redo the biscuit example with 60% of bar buyers switching from biscuits. Does it still work?
  4. Say the difference between payback period and NPV in plain words.

05

Setting a price

Three walls make a box

The most common mistake here is treating the three pricing methods like a menu and picking one. They are not a menu. Each one tells you a different edge, and your price lives in the space between them.

MethodWhat it tells youHow to work it out
Based on our costsThe lowest we can goWhat one unit costs us to make, plus a share of the setup money we want back
Based on rivalsWhere everyone else sitsWhat the closest alternative costs, adjusted up or down for how we differ
Based on valueThe highest we can goHow much money or hassle we save the customer — that is the most they would ever pay
YOUR PRICE LIVES IN HERE
FLOOR — our cost
← pick somewhere inside →
CEILING — their value

There is a fourth method, for rare cases

Sometimes all three break at once. Cost is tiny compared to what the thing is worth (software, medicine). There are no rivals, because nobody has made this before. And the value to the customer is huge but impossible to put a number on — how do you price a life-saving drug?

Then price is set purely by testing what people will actually pay. Try different prices in different places and watch what happens to sales. That is all “price sensitivity” pricing really means.

Which one leads depends on the product

  • Much like something that already exists → rivals lead, value adjusts it up or down
  • A better version of our own product → price the difference: old price plus what the improvement is worth
  • Genuinely new, nothing to compare it to → value leads, because there is no rival price to look at

Worked example — cow feed in Uttar Pradesh

A Swedish company has made a new cow feed. One bag feeds one cow for 30 days, and the cow gives 4 extra litres of milk every 10 days.

EdgeWorkingAnswer
Lowest — our costIngredients, making it, packing it, and getting it out to villages, which is expensive$100
RivalsThe feed farmers buy today$95
Highest — their value4 litres every 10 days means 12 extra litres in a 30-day bag. At $5 a litre that is $60 extra income. So a farmer would pay up to $95 + $60$155
OUR PRICE SITS BETWEEN $100 AND $155 launch around $110 to $120 · try different prices in different districts · look again in six months

Two things to say out loud, not just calculate

OUR FLOOR IS ABOVE THEIR PRICE

It costs us $100 to make. The rival sells at $95. So we can never be the cheap option.

That changes everything. We cannot win on price, so the entire sales approach has to be about proving the extra milk is real. If we cannot prove it, we have no business here.

FLAG THE SHAKY ASSUMPTION FIRST

The $60 of value assumes lab results happen on real American farms, with American cows, in American heat. They might not.

Say so before the interviewer does, and suggest the fix: run a small trial on 50 farms first and check the milk actually goes up.

What if you are given no numbers at all?

This happens often, and people panic. You do not need numbers to answer well. Say what you would work out and where you would get it:

SAY IT LIKE THIS

“I do not have figures, so let me lay out the method. I would find the floor from our cost per unit. I would find the ceiling from the money we save the customer — here that is extra milk, which I could work out from litres and the milk price. And I would check where rivals sit today. Then I would recommend a price inside that range, closer to the bottom if we want share quickly, closer to the top if we want margin. Do you have any of those figures?”

Sense check

Cost and value are two nails in the wall, and your price hangs between them. Below the cost nail, you fall. Above the value nail, nobody reaches for you. The rival price is where everyone else has already hung theirs. Someone who names only one of the three has hammered in one nail and is hoping.

CHECKPOINT 05
  1. Say which method gives the floor, which gives the ceiling, and which gives the middle.
  2. Work out the ceiling for a machine that saves a factory $20,000 a year, where the current machine costs $50,000
  3. Explain what it means when your cost floor is higher than the rival’s price.
  4. Say the “no numbers” script out loud without reading it.

Growing faster

Split revenue into three parts and attack each

The client says they want to grow 30% a year. Before you draw anything, ask one question: how fast is the market growing?

If the market grows 8% and they want 30%, they are not riding a wave. They are taking customers off someone specific. That completely changes the case — now you need to know who, and why those customers would switch.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

“Before I list ways to grow, can I check the target against the market? If the industry grows at 8% and we want 30%, most of that has to come from taking share off rivals rather than the market getting bigger. That changes where I would look.”

Split revenue into three parts

This is the single most useful thing in this section. Learn it properly.

REVENUE = HOW MANY CUSTOMERS × HOW OFTEN THEY BUY × HOW MUCH PER PURCHASE three parts, three sets of levers, four seconds to say
PartWays to push it up

Worked example — a food delivery app wants to double

Where it is today:

customers 1 million orders each 4 per month order value $30 our cut 20% revenue = 1,000,000 × 4 × 30 × 0.20 = $24 million a month

To double, they need $48 million Most people jump straight to “double the customers”. Look what happens if you nudge all three instead:

ApproachWhat has to happenRealistic?
Customers only1 million → 2 millionVery hard. Doubling a user base is a multi-year fight.

THE INSIGHT TO SAY OUT LOUD

“Doubling any single part is very hard. But small gains multiply. A quarter more customers, a quarter more orders and a quarter more spend gets us there, and each of those is a normal year’s work rather than a miracle.”

This is why splitting revenue into parts is worth doing. It turns one impossible goal into three possible ones.

Then check it is worth having

One last step people forget. Growth costs money. If you get 25% more customers by spending heavily on discounts and adverts, check that those customers are worth more than they cost — that is the LTV against CAC comparison from Day 1. Growth that loses money on every new customer is not growth. It is a slow leak.

  4. Explain why small gains in three places beat one big gain in one place.

“Synergies” just means the extra you get from joining up

It is a scary word for a simple idea. Two companies together can sometimes do things neither could alone. Three kinds:

  • Selling more — we sell our stuff to their customers, and theirs to ours. Or together we are big enough to charge a bit more.
  • Spending less — we do not need two head offices, two finance teams, two warehouses. Buying in bigger amounts gets cheaper prices.
  • Money tricks — a bigger company can usually borrow more cheaply, and sometimes pays less tax.

A quick way to value a company

You will never build a proper valuation model in a 30-minute interview. Use this shortcut, say your assumptions out loud, and ask if they are alright.

VALUE = YEARLY CASH ÷ (RETURN WE WANT − GROWTH RATE) say both percentages out loud and check them with the interviewer

Why it works, roughly: if a company throws off $15 million a year forever, and we want a 20% return, and the cash grows 15% a year, then the gap between those two rates — 5% — is what really matters. Small gap, big value.

Worked example — an online shop buys a small AI company

Asking price $250 million The buyer is a beauty products website with $5 billion of sales. The target sells AI software that reads customer calls and finds useful patterns.

THE LINE THAT SETS YOU APART

That minus $5 million is the whole point. When you buy a company and point it at your own problems, it stops earning money from its old customers. Almost nobody subtracts that. Doing it shows you are thinking about a real business, not just running a formula.

Now attack your own answer

Once you have a positive number, do not stop. Ask how much of the value comes from the company as it stands, and how much depends on the joining-up working perfectly.

A SHAKY DEAL

On its own the company is worth $900 million The joining-up adds another $500 million Total $1.4 billion, against an asking price of $1.3 billion

So the entire $100 million of safety margin depends on every bit of that $500 million actually happening. If only 80% of it lands, we have overpaid.

WHAT TO RECOMMEND

Buy it — but only with a written plan for the joining-up, with named people responsible and dates attached.

Or ask for a lower price, or a deal where part of the payment depends on the savings actually appearing.

That is an adviser’s answer, not a calculator’s.

Cutting costs

Cut where the money is, not where it is easy

Here is a case type that gets missed in most prep, and it comes up a lot: the client wants to spend less. Sometimes it arrives on its own. More often it turns up in the middle of a profit case, once you have worked out that costs are the problem.

The whole thing rests on one rule.

THE RULE

Cut where the money is, not where cutting is easy.

Saving 20% on something that is 5% of your costs saves you 1%. Saving 5% on something that is 40% of your costs saves you 2%. People always attack the small easy pile first. Do not.

Step 1 — lay the costs out and size them

Before suggesting anything, ask for the breakdown. If you are not given one, ask for it. You cannot cut sensibly without knowing where the money goes.

Take a restaurant with $10 of cost for every $10 of sales:

ingredients $3.50 ███████ staff $3 ██████ rent $2 ████ electricity $0.80 ██ everything else $0.70 █

The client wants to cut costs by 10%, so $1. Ingredients and staff together are $6.50 of the $10. That is where the $1 has to come from. Even wiping out the electricity bill completely only gets you $0.80, and you would be sitting in the dark.

Step 2 — three ways to cut anything

For every cost bucket, there are only three questions. This is your MECE split.

LeverThe questionRestaurant example
Pay less for itCan we get the same thing cheaper?Buy vegetables in bulk, change supplier, sign a longer contract for a better rate
Use less of itCan we get the same result with less?Cut food waste, smaller portions of expensive items, better shift planning so nobody stands idle
Stop doing itDo we need this at all?Drop the six dishes nobody orders, close on the quietest afternoon, stop the printed menus

Say all three for the two biggest buckets and you have already given a better answer than most people.

Step 3 — ask what breaks

This is the step that separates a good answer from a dangerous one. Every cut has a cost of its own.

CUTS THAT COME BACK TO BITE

Cheaper ingredients → food tastes worse → customers stop coming. You saved $0.50 and lost $3 of sales.

Fewer staff → slower service → bad reviews.

No maintenance → the fridge dies in summer → you lose a whole week.

SAFE CUTS

Less waste. Nobody notices, and the food is the same.

Better shift planning. Same service, fewer idle hours paid for.

Dropping dishes nobody orders. Frees up the kitchen and cuts stock.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

“Before I recommend these cuts, I want to check what each one might damage. Cutting ingredient quality saves money but risks the customer experience, which shows up in sales later. I would rank cuts by how much they save against how much they risk, and start with the ones that save real money and risk nothing.”

Two more things worth knowing

  • One-off versus every year. Selling an unused van gives you cash once. Renegotiating the rent saves you money every single year. The second is worth far more, even if the number looks smaller. Always say which kind you are proposing.
  • Fixed costs are harder to cut than variable ones. If sales halve, ingredient spending halves by itself. The rent does not. That is why a business with big fixed costs suffers badly in a bad year — and why cutting fixed costs, though painful, is often the only thing that really helps.

The full structure, in order

LIST THE COSTS → SIZE EACH ONE → ATTACK THE BIGGEST TWO
→ PAY LESS · USE LESS · STOP DOING IT → CHECK WHAT BREAKS and say whether each saving is one-off or every year

Doing the maths

Out loud, fast, without slipping a zero

You will lose more marks to slow, mumbled arithmetic than to a weak structure. The good news is that this is the most fixable thing in the whole case. It is just habits.

Habit 1 — say your unit before you start

“I will work in millions throughout.” Then stay in millions. Most mistakes in cases are not arithmetic mistakes. They are someone quietly slipping between thousands, millions and billions and ending up a thousand times out.

Habit 2 — round hard, and say you are doing it

Asking permission to round is not a weakness. It is a signal that you know what matters.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

“I will treat the population as 340 million.”

“I am rounding up here, so my answer will be slightly high — I will keep that in mind at the end.”

Habit 3 — break percentages into pieces

Never try to do 35% of 4,200 in one go. Find 10%, find 1%, then add up.

10% of 4,200 = 420 so 30% = 420 + 420 + 420 = 1,260 and 5% = half of 420 = 210 35% = 1,260 + 210 = 1,470

Habit 4 — the rule of 72

Something growing at a certain percent doubles in 72 divided by that percent, in years.

YEARS TO DOUBLE = 72 ÷ GROWTH RATE 9% growth doubles in 8 years · 12% doubles in 6 · 6% doubles in 12

Use it whenever anyone asks about five or ten years ahead. It takes two seconds and sounds like you have done this before.

Habit 5 — keep the zeros out of the way

48,000 × 52 looks horrible. It is not. Do 48 × 52 = 2,496, then put the three zeros back: 2,496,000. Take the zeros out at the start, put them back at the end.

Habit 6 — every number gets a “so what”

A number with no meaning attached scores nothing. The moment you finish a calculation, say what it tells you.

WHAT NOT TO DO

“So that gives us $70 million”

[silence]

The interviewer now has to ask “and?”, which means you did not finish the thought.

WHAT TO DO

“So that gives us $70 million Against $200 million of setup costs, we are $130 million short in year one. So the question becomes how long the client can fund that gap.”

Number, meaning, next question. Every time.

Finishing the case

The last sixty seconds decide how you are remembered

Here is a gap worth closing before the interview, not during it. Most people spend all their prep on the middle of a case and none on the end. But the last sixty seconds are what the interviewer actually remembers when they write you up afterwards.

There is a fixed shape. Learn it and use it every single time.

ANSWER → THREE REASONS → ONE RISK → ONE NEXT STEP sixty seconds · answer first, always

Answer first. Not last.

This feels wrong the first time. In school you show your working and then reveal the answer. In consulting you do the opposite — you say the answer, then explain it.

The reason is practical. The person you are advising is busy. If they only hear your first sentence, that sentence should be the useful one.

BUILDING UP TO IT

“So we looked at market size, and it was 20 million units, and then we looked at share, and we thought 5% was reasonable, and the margin was $70, which gave us $70 million, and the fixed costs were $200 million, so… so I think probably they should not enter? Unless they can wait?”

The interviewer stopped listening at “so we looked at”. And the ending sounds unsure.

ANSWER FIRST

“The client should enter, but only if they can accept losses for four years.”

Now everything after that has somewhere to attach itself. The interviewer knows where you landed and can follow how you got there.

A full worked ending

Here is the skincare case, closed properly. Read it out loud and time yourself. It should take about sixty seconds.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

The answer. “My recommendation is that the client should enter the US, but only if they are prepared to lose money for the first three to four years.”

Reason one. “The economics work eventually. Each jar earns us $70, and we need 14% of the market to cover our $200 million of setup costs. That is a big share, but it is reachable in a growing market.”

Reason two. “We have something genuinely different. Both existing players use chemical ingredients and we use natural ones, so we are not fighting them head-on on the same thing.”

Reason three. “There is nothing blocking us operationally. We can set up manufacturing, and there are no major regulatory barriers.”

The risk. “The main risk is that 14% share takes longer than four years. If the existing players cut prices when we arrive, it could take much longer, and the client needs to know that going in.”

The next step. “The first thing I would do is a small test launch in two or three cities, to check whether American customers actually pay a premium for natural ingredients before we commit $200 million to a factory.”

Why one risk, and not five

Naming one risk shows judgement. Naming five shows you cannot tell which one matters. Pick the one that would most change the answer if it turned out badly, and say only that one.

Why the next step matters so much

This is the part almost everyone drops, and it is the cheapest mark in the whole case. A recommendation with no next step is just an opinion. A recommendation with a next step is advice.

Good next steps are small, cheap and answer the biggest unknown. A pilot in two cities. A survey of 200 farmers. A call with three suppliers. Never “do more analysis” — say what analysis, and what decision it would settle.

What if you did not finish?

This happens to everyone, and how you handle it matters more than the fact of it. Do not pretend. Do not trail off. Say where you got to and what you would have done next.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

“Based on what we have covered, my view leans towards entering, driven mainly by the margin per unit. I have not yet checked whether we can actually build a distribution network, and that would be the thing that could change my answer. If it holds up, I would recommend going ahead.”

That is a confident, honest ending. It is far better than a mumbled one, and interviewers respect it.

Sense check

You can climb beautifully for twenty minutes and still be judged on how you land. The ending is the only part of the case the interviewer will repeat to somebody else. Practise it more than you think you need to — it is sixty seconds you can rehearse word for word, and almost nobody does.

CHECKPOINT 10
  1. Say the four parts of the ending in order.
  2. Explain why you give the answer first rather than last.
  3. Close the cow feed pricing case out loud in sixty seconds, using the shape.
  4. Say the “I did not finish” line so it sounds calm rather than apologetic.

Practice cases

Four prompts. Run them timed.

Reading does not make you better at cases. Running them does. Here are four prompts covering the types in this region. Run each one timed, out loud, recorded on your phone.

HOW TO RUN THESE PROPERLY

Set a timer for 20 minutes. Speak everything out loud, even the thinking. Record it. Then listen back and note two things: where you went silent for more than ten seconds, and where you said a number without saying what it meant.

Those two notes are worth more than another hour of reading.

Case 1 — a European airline looks at the US

Your client is a European low-cost airline. Growth at home has stopped. They want to look at the US. They have no profit target in mind but want steady long-term growth. Should they enter, and if so how?

Watch for: did you ask, during clarifying, whether they mean domestic US routes or international ones, and whether they want to stay low-cost? Both change the whole case. Did you remember that US aviation is a brutal industry — expensive fuel, price-sensitive customers, fierce rivals? The five forces should fire here without being asked for.

Case 2 — a coaching centre wants to double

Your client runs 12 coaching centres in Gujarat teaching school students. They want to double revenue in three years. How should they do it?

Watch for: did you split revenue into students, courses per student and fee per course? Did you check what the market is growing at before accepting the target? Did you consider online as a way to reach new students without building new centres — and then check whether online fees are lower, which would break the maths?

Case 3 — a hospital needs to cut costs

Your client is a 200-bed private hospital. Costs have risen faster than revenue for two years. The board wants a 12% cost reduction. Where would you look?

Watch for: did you ask for the cost breakdown before suggesting anything? Did you go after the two biggest buckets rather than the easy ones? Most importantly — did you check what each cut might damage? In a hospital, some cuts are not just bad business, they are dangerous. Saying that out loud matters.

Case 4 — a snack company is for sale

Your client is a large packaged foods company. A regional snack brand with $400 million of sales is for sale at $1.2 billion Should they buy it?

Watch for: did you split value into the company on its own plus the extra from joining up? Did you name all three kinds of extra value? Did you then attack your own number and ask how much of it depends on the joining-up going perfectly? And did you cover the people side, not just the money?
